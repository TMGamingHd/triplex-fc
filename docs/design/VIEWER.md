# The 3D viewer: the flight seen as a vehicle, not as numbers

> Status: **built** (ADR-035, 9 Oct 2026): `console/web/viewer/` (the page: WebGL through three.js, vendored), `console/tfc_console/viewer.py` (the data path), `sim/vehicle/viewer_state.hpp` (what the simulator sends), `tfc_simd --viewer`, `tfc_fly --pose`, and a vehicle of the Starship V3 class, `vehicles/starship.json`. Tested on the host (C++: 10 tests, a flight of the vehicle through hot staging and 14 mutants; Python: 39 tests) and **live against the real firmware** (three flight-computer processes, ACT and the simulator on `vcan0`: 50 poses a second, 59 frames a second in the browser). The pictures were looked at in a real browser on a real GPU, which is the only check a picture has; **nothing here has run on a board**, and the GPU cost has been measured on one machine only (section 13).

## 1. What it is, and what it is not

The console ([`CONSOLE.md`](CONSOLE.md)) shows the system as numbers, charts and a SVG rocket. The viewer shows the **vehicle** the simulator is flying: in a sky, over a launch site, with its engines, its stages, its air and its forces, from any side, live or from a recording, in seven **lenses**: *Broadcast* (the flight as a webcast shows it, section 20) and six that each look at one process in more detail: *Overview*, *Aerodynamics* (with the centre of pressure and the centre of mass), *Atmosphere*, *Propulsion*, *Loads* and *Attitude*.

It is a **window, not a model**. It decides nothing, sends nothing to the flight bus, and computes nothing the simulator should have computed: every number in a pose is the simulator's own state or one of its own functions (the atmosphere, the wind, the aerodynamics). Where the viewer calculates (a coefficient, a bending moment) it is a formula on those numbers and the lens says so; where it *draws* something the simulator does not model (the air's flow, the exhaust, the ground) it is labelled an illustration. Section 6 is the full list, because the project's rule is that a number says where it came from.

## 2. Starting it

```bash
console/tfc-console                       # the usual: the page opens; "3D viewer" is printed under the address
#  http://127.0.0.1:8765/viewer/?token=...     the viewer, in a window of its own (a second monitor)
console/tfc-console --viewer console/demo/starship-ascent.pose.jsonl.gz   # a recorded flight of the Starship-class vehicle: no bus, no rig
build/host/tfc_fly vehicles/starship.json --sensors vehicle --no-accel --pad 1500 --pose flight.pose.jsonl   # make your own
```

In the viewer, the chip at the top left says where the data comes from; click it to **open a recorded flight**, to **fly any vehicle in `vehicles/`** with the real flight software (it runs `tfc_fly` and plays the result: no flight computer process, a software run of the flight function). To see the **real flight computers** fly a vehicle, choose it on the Launch tab (*Vehicle on the rig*): the console builds the three computers with that vehicle's tables (about half a minute), the rig starts, and the viewer follows the launch live (ADR-036, `CONSOLE.md` section 8), or to see where a live simulator sends. Keys: `1`–`6` the lenses, `C` the next camera, `Space` play and pause, `H` hide the panels, `F` full screen, `P` a picture.

With the rig running (**Rig** tab of the console, *Closed loop with the launch sequence*), the console starts `tfc_simd` with `--viewer PORT` itself and the viewer shows the launch as it happens.

## 3. Why a page of its own, on the same server

The question was whether the viewer should share the console's page or be a different local server. **A separate page on the same server, in its own browser window.** The cost of a 3D view is the browser's main thread and the GPU, not the server's: a separate window is a separate browser process, so the console's charts and the viewer's frames do not take turns, and the rig (five processes with 10 ms deadlines, which a loaded host disturbs) is not asked for more than a pose stream a page may or may not listen to. A second *server* process would have to receive the same UDP telemetry (the port can be bound once), keep a second copy of the state and the token, and be one more thing to start; it would buy nothing the window does not. The viewer has its own event stream (`/api/pose-stream`): a console with no viewer open pays nothing for it (a pose is parsed and kept as the latest, and dropped).

```
 tfc_simd --viewer PORT  --UDP 50 Hz-->  PoseSource  --> ViewerHub --SSE /api/pose-stream--> viewer page (WebGL)
 tfc_fly --pose FILE   --file-->  PoseReplay (play, pause, seek, speed) -->   ^                  |
 a console recording (sidecar k:pose) --> LogSource (the bus replay) --------+                    +-- also /api/stream: the console's own state (nodes, ACT, mission clock)
```

## 4. What the simulator sends

`sim/vehicle/viewer_state.hpp` writes two kinds of one-line JSON; `tfc_simd --viewer PORT [--viewer-hz N]` sends them as UDP datagrams (a **pose** 50 times a second, the **spec** once at the start and every two seconds, because a datagram nobody listened to is lost); `tfc_fly --pose FILE [--pose-hz N]` writes them to a file (spec first). Both are output only: nothing the flight depends on reads them. With `--mission FILE` (both tools) the pose also carries `others`: the stage that was let go, flown on by that mission file's plan (`RECOVERY.md` section 8), so that the live rig shows the booster's return; the viewer says so with a toast and the Follow bar offers it.

**The spec** (`k: spec`): the vehicle as the simulator reads it (the same normalised JSON `tfc_fly --dump` writes: stages, tanks, the engines with the rings expanded, so that an index in a pose is an index in this list, payloads, fins, the scenario), the planet's radius, mu, rotation and pole, and the **air by altitude** every kilometre to 120 km as the model gives it (temperature, pressure, density, speed of sound, mean wind): the viewer draws the atmosphere the vehicle flew through, dispersions included.

**A pose** (`k: pose`), in the inertial frame (+X up at the launch point at T-zero, +Y downrange, +Z crossrange; the body frame has +X along the axis to the nose):

| Key | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| `t`, `tt`, `fr` | flight time (negative on the pad), the vehicle's own time, the frame | the runner |
| `r`, `v`, `q`, `w` | position (m, from the planet's centre), velocity, attitude (w, x, y, z: body to inertial), body rates (rad/s) | `Vehicle6::state()` |
| `alt`, `rng`, `spd`, `mach`, `qd`, `alpha` | altitude, arc downrange, speed, Mach, dynamic pressure, total angle of attack (deg) | `Vehicle6`, `Loads` |
| `vair`, `wind`, `air` | velocity through the air in the body frame, the wind (inertial), [T, p, rho, a] here | `wind_at`, `atmosphere` |
| `m`, `cg`, `cp`, `cna`, `ca`, `dref` | mass, centre of mass and centre of pressure (m from the tail), the normal-force slope, the axial coefficient, the reference diameter | `mass_props`, `aero_summary` |
| `fth`, `fae`, `mth`, `mae`, `thr`, `mdot` | thrust and aerodynamic force and moment about the CG (body frame), total thrust, mass flow | `Loads` |
| `eng`, `gim`, `fin`, `surf`, `sdep` | thrust fraction of each engine (-1: failed), gimbal pitch and yaw of each stage, fin deflections; `surf` is the deflection in degrees of every articulated surface (the vehicle file's `surfaces`, in its order) and `sdep` a bit mask of those deployed (only when the vehicle has surfaces) | `engine_fraction`, `stage_gimbal_*`, `fin_*` |
| `prop`, `tank` | propellant per stage and the liquid in each tank (kg) | `propellant`, `tank_liquid_kg` |
| `stg`, `ign`, `pay`, `gnd`, `clamp`, `crash` | bits of the stages on, ignited, payloads on; on the ground, clamped, destroyed | the model |
| `tilt`, `ref`, `cmd`, `act` | the two tilts, the program's tilts, ACT's command, ACT's mode | `tilts`, the flight tables, the runner |
| `slosh`, `flex` | slosh displacements and the bending mode, when the vehicle has them | the state |

The C++ tests (`tests/test_viewer_state.cpp`) hold the pose to the vehicle: the position is a planet radius plus the altitude, the quaternion is a unit one in (w, x, y, z) order, the air velocity has the wind taken out, the tanks add up to their stage's propellant, the gimbals are the stages' (pitch then yaw), a failed engine is -1, a separated stage's tanks read empty and its engines read off, and the first line is the spec of the vehicle it describes. Fourteen mutants of the export and of the accessors it uses (`tools/mutation/sim_mutations.py`) are killed.

## 5. Frames, time, and the two scenes

**Axes.** The scene's are Y up, X right, Z toward the viewer; the simulator's are X up, Y downrange, Z crossrange. The map is the cyclic permutation `scene = (sim.Z, sim.X, sim.Y)` (determinant +1, no mirror image), applied to vectors and to the vector part of quaternions, and to the vehicle's body axes (the model's Y is the vehicle's axis).

**Time.** The page keeps a clock that advances in real time at the source's speed and is pulled gently to just behind the newest pose (0.1 s); poses between are interpolated (a cubic through both positions and velocities, a slerp for the attitude, a line for the rest), so a late datagram never shows as a jump. A seek in a recording resets the page and shows the pose it landed on.

**Two scenes, because the numbers do not fit in one.** A planet is 6,371 km across and a vehicle is 9 m. In one scene with one depth buffer, 32-bit floats cannot put a bolt and a horizon in the same frame. So the sky, the atmosphere and the planet are one fragment shader that works from the camera's place above the planet's centre (in kilometres), and everything near is drawn after it, in a scene whose origin is the vehicle's centre of gravity, in metres, with a depth buffer of its own: what is within a kilometre of the camera has millimetre precision (the transforms are done in JavaScript's doubles and only the small offsets reach the GPU).

**The sky** is single scattering by Rayleigh and Mie particles with exponential density (scale heights 8 and 1.2 km): the blue sky, the red sunset, the white glow round the sun and the thin bright limb seen from orbit come out of it, and its thickness follows the simulator's planet. The planet's surface is **procedural** (a coast at the launch point, the sea downrange, land behind, drifting clouds, ice, lights on the night side): it is not a map of anywhere unless an imagery pack is present (section 12). The vehicle is lit by the sun (its colour is the atmosphere's transmittance along the sun's path) and by the sky itself, which is rendered into a small cube at the vehicle's place a few times a second and filtered for its reflections and ambient light, so a steel tank reflects the real sky, the real ground or the black of space.

## 6. What is measured, derived and illustrative

| Lens | Measured (the simulator's own) | Derived (a formula on it; the lens says which) | Illustrative (a picture, not a result) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overview** | altitude, speed, Mach, q, mass, thrust, the stages' propellant, the engines running, the events | acceleration in g, thrust-to-weight, vertical speed | the sky, the ground, the launch site and its tower, the exhaust trail, the pad's dust, a stage that has been let go (carried on by the viewer; the simulator does not track it) |
| **Aerodynamics** | Mach, angle of attack, the air, the forces and moments, the centre of mass, the centre of pressure | C_N, C_A, C_D, C_L, L/D, Reynolds number, stagnation temperature, Sutton-Graves nose heating (an assumed 0.5 m nose radius), the static margin | the pressure on the skin (modified Newtonian: Cp_max cos² of the angle to the wind, Cp_max from Rayleigh's pitot formula: a hypersonic method, qualitative below Mach 3), the streamlines (slender-body potential flow from the vehicle's own radius profile), the bow shock (the Taylor-Maccoll cone shock of the nose's angle, tilted with the angle of attack), the Prandtl-Glauert cloud (humidity assumed) |
| **Atmosphere** | pressure, density, temperature, speed of sound, the mean wind (the model's own, every km) | viscosity (Sutherland), mean free path, Knudsen number, scale height, the deviation from the 1976 standard | the shells in the sky and the colours of the bands |
| **Propulsion** | every engine's thrust fraction, the propellant in every tank, the mass flow, the gimbal | effective specific impulse, pressure loss, burn time left, delta-v left (Tsiolkovsky with the effective Isp) | the colour of the liquid, a tank drawn as a cylinder (the simulator's model of it), how far a plume spreads (the nozzle's exit pressure is not modelled: 70 kPa for a sea-level bell and 15 kPa for a vacuum one are assumed) |
| **Loads** | q, angle of attack, forces and moments, masses, where they are | the compression, shear and bending moment at 160 stations: a lumped-mass estimate (the mass spread along each stage and in each tank at its level, every piece accelerating as a rigid body, the aerodynamic normal force at the centre of pressure and the drag along the length): **not a structural analysis**, and there is no allowable | the curve drawn beside the vehicle |
| **Attitude** | the tilts, the rates, the program, the gimbals, ACT's command and mode, and (from the console) the nodes' health and ACT's vote | the error as an angle between two axes | the wire ghost of the vehicle at the program's attitude |

## 7. The vehicle's model

A vehicle is drawn from its **vehicle file** as the simulator read it: the diameters and the nose of every stage, the engines where the engine list puts them, the tanks. A generated model has three styles: *painted* (any vehicle), *Starship* and *plain*. **Starship** is a stainless 9 m stack: weld rings, a booster with its hot-stage ring and vents, the grid fins (the vehicle file's: two, one for each side the tower takes the stage by) stowed against the skin and two catch pins, an engine skirt and a thrust plate; a ship with heat-shield tiles on the belly and the nose, two forward and two aft flaps stowed, and its engines (three sea-level, three vacuum, which have larger bells). Its proportions are the class's, public figures, not drawings, and **no markings are drawn**.

`vehicles/starship.json` is a vehicle of the **Starship V3 (Block 3) class** for it to be. It is built from **publicly reported numbers and my estimates, and is not SpaceX data**, not a model of any flight article, and nothing in the project's claims depends on it: it exists so that the simulator and the viewer have a vehicle of that class to fly. Where each number comes from (searched 9 Oct 2026; the sources disagree in places, and the column says which choice was made):

Flown with the real flight software (`tfc_fly`, the vehicle's own sensors, a 15 s pad phase so that the gyros are calibrated, re-measured on the final file, 11 Oct 2026) it reaches **max-Q of 31.8 kPa at 64.1 s** (Flight 12 reported max-Q at T+45 s: the program is the mission's, not a copy of that flight's), the booster's engines cut off at 132 s and it is let go at 134 s at **44.4 km and 1.53 km/s**, and the ship cuts off at 470 s and is at **142 km and 7.94 km/s** at 491 s (**a perigee of 64 km and an apogee of 656 km**; the attitude error against the program is 1.2° rms and 5.7° at worst). Through the *rig* (three real flight computers built for it by the Launch tab, ACT, the simulator, `vcan0`, 11 Oct 2026): all three computers healthy and in Triplex to the end, **the booster flown on by the host (`RECOVERY.md` section 8) and caught by the tower** (`cgt` 1 at about 397 s), and the viewer's Follow bar offering it. What the viewer cannot show for want of data: the real vehicle's aerodynamics (the simulator's slender-body model), its structure and its engines' real gimbal limits.

**Adjusting the Starship.** Two places. The *physics* is the vehicle file (and the Vehicle tab's knobs). The *picture's details* are the **Starship panel** of the Overview lens (shown when the vehicle is drawn as a Starship): the hot-stage ring's height; it is remembered by the browser and changes nothing about the flight. **The flaps and the grid fins are not a picture choice** (ADR-041, ADR-043, ADR-045, ADR-046): they are `surfaces` of `vehicles/starship.json` (two grid fins on the booster, four flaps on the ship), drawn by `models/flaps.js` at the deflection and the deployed bit the simulator reports in every pose. `?flapdeg=40` and `?finsout=1` draw every surface at that deflection, or deployed, to look at them without flying a return.

* **The ship's flaps** are hinged along the body's axis and stand straight out for the climb (stow 90 degrees: the owner's description of the real ship, and a forum report that the real ones sit edge-on to the airflow on ascent; no official statement and no dimensions were found): two small flaps below the nose, two large ones at the tail, each a **flat, thick plate in a trapezoidal outline** (a long hinge edge, a shorter outer edge, a swept leading edge, cut corners; the aft pair large and swept, the forward pair smaller and more pointed) with the tile face and the bare steel face (weld lines and ribs), and a low hinge (a barrel, two brackets and a half-shell fairing). They are on **either side of the heat shield**, which is centred on the belly (azimuth 0): the aft pair at 80 and 280 degrees, the forward pair at 112 and 248. Edge-on to the climb's stream they add only their friction (about 6 % more drag, `AERODYNAMICS.md` section 9); a mission that turns them (`channel`, `deploy_surfaces`) turns the plate in the stream and the picture shows the same angle.
* **The booster's two grid fins** (one on each flank, at 96 and 264 degrees, for the sides the tower takes the stage by) are **lattices whose face is across the body's axis**, so that with the stage upright they lie **parallel to the ground**: a squared-off frame round a dense square lattice (12 cells across, 8 along the radius, open pockets the air goes through), raw dark metal with a weld bead along the joints, each on a **mounting pod** (a rounded structural fairing on a reinforced base with a doubler plate, housing the actuator). They fold along the skin toward the tail and stand out when the pose says `deployed`, easing over 1.5 s. **They rest on the catch pins**: each pod carries a pin out of its outer face 1.5 m under the fin, at the height the file gives (`catch_pin_x_m` 61 m of 72.3; the fins' hinge is at 62.5 m), and the tower's arms take the stage there.

**A CAD model of your own.** Put a glTF file (`.glb`) in `console/models/` and choose it under *Model* (`console/models/README.md`). The viewer asks which way the vehicle points in the file, scales it to the vehicle file's length by default and puts its tail at the origin, so that the engines, the centre of mass, the tanks and every overlay of the lenses (all of which come from the vehicle file and the simulator) are where the picture has them. Nodes named `stage1`, `stage2`… leave with their stage. The engines' bells of an imported picture are its own; the plumes and the gimbal are the vehicle file's.

## 8. Sources, and how a flight is kept

* **Live.** `tfc_simd --viewer PORT` (the rig the console starts gets it). The chip says *live simulator* and the bottom bar the pose rate.
* **A recording.** The console's recording (**REC**) writes `k: pose` and `k: spec` lines into the sidecar beside the bus log, so the bus replay plays the poses with the rest and a seek shows the viewer only where it ended (about 2.6 MB a minute, before gzip; the demo is kept compressed).
* **A pose file.** `tfc_fly VEHICLE --pose FILE` (`.pose.jsonl`, or `.gz`): `console/tfc-console --viewer FILE`, or the chip's menu. It has its own transport (play, pause, ±10 s, a scrubber, speed from 0.25× to 16×).
* **An older simulator or a recording made before the viewer existed** sends only the console's 10 Hz telemetry: the viewer then places and turns the vehicle (a generic one) and says so (*telemetry only*): there are no forces, no air and no per-engine state in it, and the lenses say what they miss.

## 9. CFD

The viewer's own flow pictures (the streamlines, the shock cone, the transonic cloud, the Newtonian pressure map) are illustrations and say so. Since ADR-050 there is also a **CFD view** in the Aerodynamics lens that shows what an Euler solver computed on the vehicle's own outline (`CFD.md`): a section through the flow (the Mach number, the pressure, or a schlieren picture) and the pressure on the skin, for the case nearest the flight's Mach number and angle of attack, with the solver's CN-alpha and centre of pressure beside the engineering build-up's. The files are `console/cfd/**/*.cfd` and `console/demo/*.cfd` (`tfc_cfd --field`, `tools/cfd/make_library.py`), served as `/api/viewer/cfd` and `/api/viewer/cfd/file?name=` (a name from the list only; `console/tfc_console/cfdlib.py`); the decoder and the section are `js/cfd.js`, the skin's pressure is a lookup in `flow.js`'s `CpSurface` (`setCfd`). The live solver (`js/gpuflow.js`, `CFD.md` section 10) is an axisymmetric Euler solver in WebGL2 fragment shaders that follows the flight's Mach number and draws the same section; `gputest.html?token=...` checks it against Taylor-Maccoll on the page (the numbers go to the console server's log), and `gtcheck.html?token=...&m=./js/NAME.js,...` imports modules and prints a syntax error on the page, since a headless browser has no console and an ES module's parse error does not reach `window.onerror`. A library case is a computed case, not a live flow: nothing is interpolated between cases and the flight does not use any of it. The seams that remain for a CFD result are the **streamlines** (`Streamlines.build`, today an integration through an analytic field: a velocity field on a grid to integrate through) and the **shock** (a surface from a density-gradient iso-surface), neither done.

## 10. Cameras

*Orbit* (drag to turn round the vehicle, wheel to zoom, right-drag to slide along it; double-click to go back to the start), *Chase*, *Tracking* (a camera on the ground by the pad that follows it up, with a field of view that keeps it the same size), *Engines* (on the vehicle, looking at the plumes), *Nose*, *Earth* (looking down at the planet it is leaving), *Ground* and *Wide*. They are a bar at the bottom of the screen in every lens (they were a card of the Overview lens, which made them invisible in the other five), with what the current one does under it. **Ground** is a person 380 m from the pad who *looks at the vehicle* wherever it goes, with a lens that zooms to keep it and its plume in the picture; a drag turns the view away from it, the wheel zooms, a double-click brings it back (it used to look in one fixed direction and the vehicle was usually out of the picture, behind a panel). **Wide** looks from the side at the middle of the line from the pad to the vehicle, far enough back to hold both. A vehicle too small to see (in the Tracking, Wide, Ground and Earth views) is circled and labelled with its altitude and speed. The near plane of the camera follows the distance to what it looks at (0.2 %, at least 0.3 m): with a fixed 0.3 m a 24-bit depth buffer resolves 40 m at 14 km and a vehicle seen from there was a blob. The lenses set a camera that suits them (the stability view is a side view of the whole vehicle).

## 11. The stage that has been let go

The simulator drops a stage when it separates: it does not track it. The viewer carries the picture of it on with the speed it had, pushes it back by the separation's delta-v, lets gravity and a simple drag act on it and turns it slowly, until it is under the ground or ten minutes old. That is a picture, not a result (a booster that flips back and lands is not drawn), and the Overview says so.

## 12. Earth imagery

The planet can wear pictures of the real Earth instead of the procedural one. Nothing of it is in the repository: `console/tfc-imagery` downloads a pack of about 13 MB into `console/imagery/` (git-ignored) from NASA's GIBS web map service, which needs no key and whose imagery is public domain, and writes a manifest that says what each file is, where it is on the Earth and where it came from (`tfc_console/imagery.py`, the docstring says why these layers). With no pack the viewer draws the procedural planet it always drew, and the Overview lens says how to get one.

| Layer | What it is | Pixel | Memory on the GPU (RGBA8 with mip maps, computed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earth 4K | Blue Marble Next Generation: a cloud-free composite of MODIS (500 m source) | 9.8 km at the equator | 45 MB |
| Earth 8K | the same at twice the size | 4.9 km | 179 MB |
| Night lights | Black Marble (VIIRS), 4K | 9.8 km | 45 MB |
| Site, regional | Landsat WELD annual composite, 4° × 4° round the pad (the United States only) | 108 m | 90 MB |
| Site, local | the same, 1° × 1° | 27 m (the sensor's own 30 m) | 90 MB |

The shader puts them on the planet from the longitude and latitude of each point (the pole and the pad's direction give the axes; the picture is placed at the launch site nearest in latitude to the simulator's launch point, which has a latitude and no longitude: 28.5° is Kennedy Space Center; the sites are `SITES` in `imagery.py`), takes the sampling gradients from the same coordinates with the jump of the date line taken out, so that the mip level is right at the seam, and layers the three scales (the 108 m and 27 m pictures fade in over their edges). The picture's colour is kept for the land and the sea (shoals, turbidity), the sea is found as what is bluer than it is red and dark, and is given the viewer's own sun glint; a picture magnified past its texels is given fine grain (the viewer's, not the picture's). The night side shows the Black Marble lights where the sea is not. The atmosphere, the clouds and the exposure are drawn over it as before. The near scene's flat ground (made up, with a coast of its own) gives way to the picture beyond a few hundred metres of the pad, so that the real ground is what the vehicle climbs over (seen at 3 km: the roads, the ponds and the marsh of the Cape); on the pad itself the apron and the ground round it are still the viewer's, and the picture is the horizon.

![The launch complex of the Cape from 0 m and from 3 km with the 8K and site imagery: the pad's own ground, and the real roads, ponds and marsh beyond it](img/viewer-imagery.jpg)

**What it is not.** A picture of the ground, not terrain: there is no height. The Landsat composite is **of the year 2000** (the newest annual composite the service returned with pixels over Florida: 2008 and 2010 came back empty), so the Cape is the Cape of that year, and the pads are not today's. Blue Marble is a monthly composite and the clouds in the viewer are its own, not the weather of the day. Outside the United States the picture is the 4.9 km or 9.8 km globe and nothing finer. Choices are in the Overview lens (*Imagery*: best there is, procedural, 4K, 8K, with the site, and the night lights); `?img=MODE` and `?night=0` set them from the address.

**Which looks and performs best, measured** (`?bench=1`: each choice in turn, the same frame drawn 100 times with the GPU made to finish each one, the median and the 95th percentile; the development machine of section 13, a 1280 × 720 canvas, one lens (Overview), three views: 600 km above the pad looking 50° from the vertical, 15 km up looking down the coast, and on the pad):

|---|---|---|---|
| Procedural | 6 ms, 9 ms | 7 ms, 11 ms | 5 ms, 8 ms |
| Earth 4K | 5 ms, 7 ms | 5 ms, 8 ms | 5 ms, 6 ms |

**The pictures cost nothing the procedural planet did not**: a texture fetch is cheaper than the noise the procedural surface evaluates (so the viewer is a little faster with imagery), and the sky pass remains the cost. What they cost is **GPU memory and load time**: the most detailed choice holds about 400 MB of textures (8K day, night and two patches), which a laptop with a shared GPU may not like, and the pictures have to be fetched and decoded at the first choice: **50 to 70 ms for each choice on this machine** (from the server on the same machine; the benchmark polls every 50 ms, so that is an upper bound with a floor of 50), after which the first frames draw them. **Not measured**: the upload to the GPU as a cost of its own (it happens in the frame the picture first appears in, which the benchmark does not isolate), and the fetch over a real network. To the eye (the comparison was made at 350 km, 45° from the vertical): the procedural planet is a green plain with clouds, plainly made up; the 4K globe is right at orbital heights and soft below 300 km; the 8K globe is visibly sharper at the coasts; the site pictures are the difference between a map and a place from a few hundred kilometres down (in the 27 m picture at 15 km: a runway, the roads, the marsh channels and the edge of each field). **The recommendation is "Earth 8K + the launch site" where the GPU has the memory, "Earth 4K" where it has not**, and the viewer's default, *best there is*, takes the first. **Not measured**: a GPU other than the development one, and any browser other than Firefox. (A first attempt at the pad view reported nothing; it was the run, not the page: the same benchmark, rerun alone, reports in every view.)

## 13. Cost, and what has not been measured

On the development machine (an NVIDIA GTX 1660 SUPER, Firefox 157, a 1600×900 window) the viewer holds 60 frames a second (the display's rate) with all six lenses; the sky pass is the cost (about 30 samples a pixel, a lower number in the cube that lights the vehicle). The ribbon streamlines of the Flow view (ADR-041) and the flaps' and grid fins' meshes (ADR-043) were added after that measurement and **their cost has not been measured**: `?bench=1` times the Earth imagery only, and a headless browser renders in software, where only ratios mean anything. That is one machine: **no frame time has been measured on any other GPU**, and the *quality* setting that lowers the sky's samples and the resolution exists because a laptop will need it. The rig is sensitive to a loaded host; the viewer is a second browser process, and **starting a headless browser during a live rig run has cost a frame and a cascade of latches before** (`CONSOLE.md` section 8): open the viewer before a run, not during one.

There is no JavaScript engine on the bench, so the page's code is checked by three things that need none: every import of every module resolves to a file, the vendored library is the release the README names (SHA-256), no script is inline (the policy forbids it); and by looking at it. The page also has a smoke test of its own, as the console has (`?selftest=1&hold=30000`, in a headless Firefox): it goes through the six lenses, the eight cameras and the three model styles on the flight being shown and checks that nothing threw, that every lens drew its panels with numbers and no NaN, and that a camera has a finite eye (38 checks, all passing on 9 Oct 2026). A headless browser does not put a WebGL canvas into its screenshots, so the page has `?shot=N` (after N frames, the canvas is copied into an image over itself) and `?debug=1` (its console goes to the server's standard error); the recipe is in the project's notes.

## 14. What it does not claim

* It is a **bench and design tool**. Its sky is procedural, its Starship is the class's proportions, its flow is a picture.
* It does not know what the flight computers decided; it shows the state of the vehicle they steer and, with the rig, the console's own view of them.
* **The centre of pressure it draws is the body's.** The plant's `aero_summary` (the slope of the normal force and its moment) is the body's and the fins' of the geometry; the flaps and grid fins are surfaces with their own loads, added to the force and the moment at every step, and are **not** in the slope: with the flaps standing out or held at 60 degrees the drawn centre of pressure is a little off the true one (the force and the moment arrows and the static margin's sign for the body alone are unaffected). A slope from the surfaces' loads (a difference of two angles of attack) is the fix and is not made.
* The ship's belly entry (`starship_ship_return.json`, ADR-044) is flown and drawn (the flaps stand at their trim and steer about it), but the ship does not land: its pins are not drawn on the arms and nothing takes it.
* The Loads lens is not stress analysis: it has no allowables and no margins, because the project has none.
* **A mutant of the pictures does not exist**: what the viewer draws is checked by eye, by the tests of the data it draws from, and by a list of what each picture is (section 6).

## 15. What the screen shows, and how it is kept readable (ADR-041)

The first version put two docks of cards on both sides, every one open, and every overlay of a lens on at once; a Flow view of the aerodynamics was a sheet of white lines and three labels on one point. What changed (11 Oct 2026, after the owner called the lenses cluttered, the ground view buggy and the colours poor):
* **Cards fold** (click the header; remembered per title). Reference cards ("Where the numbers come from") and every chart after the first start folded; the Overview's camera, Earth, model and sun controls are one folded card, "Scene". Each dock can be hidden from the edge of the screen, and a narrow window starts without the right one. The bottom of a dock fades instead of being cut.
* **A view of the aerodynamics tells one story.** *Flow*: streamlines, the shock, the relative wind, the angle of attack. *Pressure*: the pressure on the skin. *CP and CG*: the forces and the stability. The other views' layers are off unless asked for ("Also draw, in this view"). The shock is a thin bright rim that fades down the cone (it was a sheet over the vehicle). Labels that would sit on one another are pushed apart on the screen (`declutter` in `gfx.js`, in the aerodynamics and attitude lenses).
* **Colour.** One full-spectrum scale ("turbo": dark blue, cyan, green, yellow, red) is used for the skin pressure, the streamlines and the legends, instead of a blue-white-red ramp whose middle was white. The skin's colour is stretched (a power of 0.6) so that the small differences over most of the vehicle show; its ends are the stagnation value and the suction. The **streamlines are ribbons** (2.2 px, soft-edged, fading with distance from the body), released in rings ahead of the nose so that they bend over it, and coloured by **the pressure the skin below them feels** (the modified Newtonian value of the nearest station, falling with the square of the distance from the body), because the potential flow's own speed hardly changes along a line and could not colour them. The shock is magenta. Still an illustration, as section 6 says: not a flow solution.
* **Charts** never get an axis of "73.5M, 73.5M, 73.5M": a range is at least 2 % of the size of the numbers.
* **Checked** by the in-page selftest (`?selftest=1`: 52 checks: every lens and camera, that the vehicle is inside the picture for the five cameras that should show it, that the camera bar and the locator agree, that the flaps exist and turn, that a card folds) and by looking at the pictures in a real browser; the GPU cost was not re-measured.

## 16. A stage that returns, and the stack that is smaller after a separation (ADR-042)

**Every lens measured the stack for the whole flight.** After the booster had left, the aerodynamics lens still drew the centre of pressure, the Reynolds number, the shock and the forces for 124 m of vehicle, the loads lens spread the ship's mass over the booster's stations, the attitude lens drew a ghost of the stage that was gone, the camera aimed at the middle of the stack, and the exhaust trail came from where the booster's tail had been. They now ask the spec for **`extent(stg, pay)`**: the part that is still there (from the lowest x to the highest of the stages whose bits are set, and the widest section among them), and everything that is a length, a diameter, a station or a tail uses it. The camera also compensates for the jump of the scene's origin (the centre of mass moves by tens of metres when a stage goes) and eases to its new place over about 2.5 s: the separation used to cut.

**The stage that returns is a vehicle, not scenery.** Until now the viewer let go of a stage as a tumbling copy on a ballistic path with a fixed small separation speed (`Debris` in `fx.js`), which is right for a stage nobody follows. In a mission world (`tfc_mission`, `docs/design/GNC.md`) the simulator flies the booster under its own flight computers through its flip, boost-back, entry and landing burn. `tfc_mission --pose FILE --pose-others` writes, in every pose of the main body, the whole pose of every other body (`others: [{name, stage, pose}]`), and the viewer draws each as a vehicle of its own (`Spec.sub(stage)` gives the stage's spec, its engines and surfaces; its plumes burn when its engines do; it is held by the catch arms when the pose says `cgt`). A stage that has a body of its own is not also drawn as debris. **Follow** (a bar over the cameras, only when there is a second body; `?follow=booster` from the address) turns every lens and camera to the other body: its altitude, speed, loads and forces, its engines, its pad. The source menu has **Fly a whole mission** (`/api/viewer/fly` with `{mission}`: `tfc_mission` on a file of `vehicles/missions/`, about 10 s), and the pose file it writes is an ordinary one.

**The booster lands on another pad.** `starship_return.json` puts the catch tower **400 m crosswind of the launch mount** (it was 45 m, inside the launch tower's own footprint, and the viewer drew the booster coming down onto the launch mount). The site offset is the mission's own (`mission.site.offset_y_m` downrange, `offset_z_m` crosswind, `arm_height_m`); the viewer draws a second pad there (a mount, a tower with its arms at that height, an apron) when the offset is over 60 m. **Measured** with the real flight computers' tables: the booster is caught by the tower on 7 of 8 noise seeds at 400 m, exactly as on the 45 m site (seed 3 crashes on both, 5.4 m from the site), the ship reaches 234 x 257 km; the catch is no worse for the distance because the landing guidance takes its target from the mission and nothing else.

**Still not done:** the ship's landing and catch (its deorbit and belly entry are flown in the experimental `starship_ship_return.json`, ADR-044), a Raptor-by-Raptor boost-back relight picture (the pose says how many engines burn, the plumes show it), (the hot-stage ring is not on the list any more: the V3 booster's hot staging is integrated and there is no ring to jettison), and the same for the rig: the live rig flies the stack only, the returning stage is a host-only mission.

## 17. The arms, the flaps and a catch that is margin and not luck (ADR-043)

**The grid fins lie parallel to the ground; the flaps are flat plates (ADR-045).** A grid fin is drawn as the pictures of Falcon and Super Heavy show it: a flat lattice whose face (`chord_m` across the tangent by `span_m` out along the radius, the file's area) is across the body's axis and whose cells open along the axis, the way the air goes when the stage falls. With the stage upright the deployed fin is therefore **parallel to the ground** (it was drawn as a vane standing in the plane of the axis, which is what the owner saw as "perpendicular"), and a deflection tilts it about its shaft, which is what the plant's lift is. It folds about the tangent to lie along the skin toward the tail, on a pod whose pin is **1.5 m under the fin** (the file's `x_hinge_m` 62.5 against `catch_pin_x_m` 61.0), so the fins rest on the arms that take the pins. A ship's flap is a **flat, thick plate in the outline the pictures give** (a long hinge edge, a shorter outer edge, a swept leading edge and cut corners; the aft flaps large and swept, the forward ones smaller and more pointed), with the tile face and the bare steel face and a low hinge with a half-shell fairing; it was a curved shell wrapped round the hull, which looked like a scoop. The heat shield is centred on the body's +y, the belly, which is azimuth 0 of the surfaces table and the side that meets the air in the entry (the tiles were drawn 90 degrees away, on the flaps' side, until the owner said so, 11 Oct 2026: `lathe`'s angle 0 is +Z and pi/2 is +X); the flaps are on either side of it. The booster has two grid fins, one for each side the tower takes the stage by (the owner). The sizes are the file's (`chord_m`, `span_m`); the outlines are my reading of the pictures and the owner's description, not drawings.

**The tower's arms** are drawn where the file says the stage is taken (`mission.site.arm_height_m` for the pins' height, 11 Oct 2026: raised by the launch mount's height, because the simulator's ground is the level of the launch tail and the picture's is the mount's height below it): the tower stands **downrange** of the stage and its two beams reach to the stage's axis and pass either side of it, a pin's width outside the hull, with a cup under each flank pin, on a carriage at that height. The ground is placed from the spec's `cg0_m` (the stack's centre of mass at T-zero), not from the first pose a viewer happens to see: seeking, or joining a flight late, used to move the whole scene by 30 m. They are 5 m further apart while the stage comes down between them and **close on the pins** (about 2.5 s) when the pose says the stage is held (`cgt`). A catch pad has no launch mount under it: the stage hangs in the arms over bare apron, as the real one does.

**Why the catch works on 97 % of seeds and not 89 %.** The ship's flaps changed the stack's ascent by a hair and the booster's return, tuned at the edge of the plateau of one knob, fell off it: the default noise realization missed by 6 m after overshooting the pad by a kilometre at the start of the landing burn. The knob is `bias_m` of the boost-back (how far beyond the pad the burn aims, so that the entry's drag brings it back); scanned over 20 seeds at each value: 300 gives 14 caught, 450 18, **550, 600, 650, 700 all 20**, 850 18, 1000 19, 1150 12, 1300 9, 1500 7. It is now 625, the middle of the plateau, and on 60 seeds that played no part in choosing it (21 to 80) **58 were caught**. The fins then moved to the T layout (the owner's description) and the plateau came down to 19 of 20; the catch's sink speed at the arms is 1.6 to 2.1 m/s against the arms' limit of 2.0 and the profile aims 1.5 m below the arms, so that scan was redone for `aim_below_m` (0.6: 17 of 20, 0.9: 19, **1.2, 1.8, 2.2: 20 each**) and it is 1.8: on 80 seeds not used for either choice (20 to 99) **78 are caught**. The plateaus are narrow: the catch is a tuned thing, and says so.

## 18. A graphics pass: the inside, the pad that lets go, shadows (ADR-047)

The owner, 11 Oct 2026: "make the graphics as best as possible"; the ship's flaps thicker and the top two higher up the nose; the booster, once it is coming down, short of details; "in orbit view there is no inside of the ship"; the pads and the struts that hold the stack, which should collapse at lift-off "like a real launch"; and a tiny spin for stability at lift-off. What was done, and what each part is drawn from:

* **Inside (the button on the camera bar, key `I`, `?inside=1`).** The half of the skin toward the camera is clipped away (a plane through the vehicle's axis that follows the camera round, so that the cut always faces you) and the inside is drawn (`models/interior.js`): every **tank of the file** as a stainless shell with domed ends and the **liquid in it at the level the simulator has** (`pose.tank`: oxidiser pale blue, fuel amber; the booster is seen emptying, the stage that returns is seen with what it kept), the **thrust structure** (a cone of 18 ribs from the engine plate), the **downcomer**, and, in the ship, two **header tanks** and the **payload** (a stack of 18 flat satellites that leaves when the pose says the payload has gone). It works for the main body and for a stage that has a body of its own (each model has its own materials and its own plane). The tanks' places and sizes and the liquid are the simulator's; the ribs, the downcomer, the header tanks and the satellites are drawn from knowledge of the class, not from drawings.
* **The pad lets go.** The launch mount is a table on six braced legs over a flame trench, with **twenty hold-down clamps** round the booster's tail (a base, an arm that reaches over the tail ring, a hook and a ram) and **two quick-disconnect booms** from the tower to the stack (the booster's and the ship's lines). When the pose says the vehicle is no longer clamped (`pose.clamp`), the clamps **fall away outward and down**, a little out of step with one another, and the booms **swing back**, in about a second and a half; held again (a seek back to before lift-off), they close. The tower has catwalk decks, the rails of the carriage, a crane and truss arms; the arms (the chopsticks) are **open, swung out about their roots, until a stage is held**: the launch tower's wide, the catch tower's by a few metres at the tip, and they close on the pins when the pose says the stage is held (`cgt`). The tank farm has spheres on legs as well as tanks; the apron has expansion joints and a shade for each slab.
* **Shadows.** The sun's shadow falls on the vehicle (a flap on the hull, the tower on the stack at lift-off) and on the pad's structures: a 4,096 square map over the vehicle and what is beside it (the ground, which is a shader of its own, does not take shadows).
* **The skin.** Every ring of sheets of the hull is a shade of its own (rolled and welded from different coils), the hot-stage ring is proud of the skin with a rim under it, the booster has a **raceway** (the cable and line fairing) down the side away from the fins, the heat shield has a darker edge strip where the tiles end, the ship's nose has four small thruster pods, the Raptors are dark bronze, and the heat shield **glows** in an entry (the stagnation heating from the density and speed of the pose, Sutton-Graves with a 0.4 m nose, from 0.2 to 3 MW/m²: orange emission on the skin).
* **The flaps.** Thicker (0.9 m), and the forward pair moved up the nose (the file's `x_hinge_m` 107 m, straddling the start of the nose cone at 108.3 m).
* **The roll at lift-off** is in the plant, not the picture: `vehicle.roll_rate_dps` (`VEHICLE_SPEC.md`) imposes a roll that goes out to 30 degrees and back between 10 s and 60 s in `starship.json` (the rig, `tfc_fly`), and the Starship mission rolls 12 degrees under its real attitude control. The picture shows what the pose's attitude says. (A roll of 45 degrees makes 4.4 degrees rms of tilt error in the firmware's two-angle controller, 30 make 2.6; a steady spin of 1 degree a second through the burn loses the vehicle, because the gimbal planes turn away from the pad's.)

* **The outside is closed at both ends** (the owner, 11 Oct 2026: "the outside needs to look complete"). The booster's top was an open tube and the ship's tail an open skirt, so that a stage coming down, or a ship seen from behind, showed its paper-thin wall and its inside: the booster has a closed, slightly domed deck with a raised lip, the ring where the ship's engines sat and a ring of vents; the ship has an inner skirt wall, the steel's thickness at the rim and the tiles of the heat shield on the plate under its engines. The raceway and the rims are plain steel (the skin's steel has vertex colours, which a box or a ring does not carry: they were black).

What is not done: the interior of the booster has no engine plumbing beyond the thrust structure and the downcomer; the cutaway is a half-section by a plane, with no cap on the cut (the inside of the far wall is what is seen behind the tanks); the pad's ground takes no shadows; **no frame time has been measured** with the shadows and the cutaway (the machine is one GTX 1660 SUPER; a software renderer's milliseconds mean nothing).

## 19. Flap shapes, a separation that is seen, arms on the descent (ADR-048)

The owner, 11 Oct 2026: the top canards on the slant of the nose, a triangular fin; the bottom a thicker smooth fin; the booster "just kind of disappears" at separation; the arms set as the rocket comes down; the booster off once it has landed.

* **The flaps.** `flapShape()` draws the outline (aft: a rounded swept trapezoid; forward: a triangle with small rounded corners) and `smoothFlap()` extrudes it with a bevel, splitting the faces by normal into tile (outside), steel (inside) and edge meshes: the aft flap is about 1.4 m thick and smooth, the canard 0.3 m with a 0.2 m bevel. The canards sit in a group tilted 0.15 rad about the tangent so that their hinge follows the nose's slant; their `x_hinge_m` is 110.0 m in the file. The shapes are my reading of the owner's description and pictures, and the depth of the lattice is a guess.
* **Separation.** The stand-in booster no longer jumps to the mission's nominal state: `PoseExtra::shift` carries the offset and eases it to zero over 20 s, so that the stage leaves the stack continuously (screens at 133, 137, 145 and 155 s: together, a ship-length apart, a dot).

## 20. A broadcast, a director, a gyro and a CFD view that is smooth (ADR-052)

The owner, 18 Oct 2026: "make sure the UI for the new CFD is smooth and looks the best it can. Also add some sort of gyro movement of the spacecraft. Look at how SpaceX shows the Starship launches, their UI is super simple and clean." Public webcasts are the model for the *layout* only: nothing of any company's is copied (no logo, no name, no typeface: the page uses the system's fonts), and the numbers are this simulator's.

**What the screen showed before it was changed** (screenshots of the mission flight at T+60, 110, 160 s; the first look is where the faults were found):
* the CFD view's *computed case* never loaded for a mission file (the card said "Case none"): the library's cases are for `starship-v3-class` and the mission file's vehicle is `starship-v3-class-return`, and the match was an exact string. A case now belongs to a vehicle by its base name (`sameVehicle` in `cfd.js`: the part before a colon, without a `-return` or `-ship-return`);
* the text said that the flight does not use the CFD, true until ADR-051 and wrong since;
* the depth slider's number wrapped into four lines; the picture jumped when the case nearest the flight changed; the vehicle was seen at a slant (the orbit camera is in the local horizon's frame and the section is in the vehicle's), cropped by the docks, in six cards of numbers that have nothing to do with the flow.

**Broadcast** (`lenses/broadcast.js`, key `0`, the lens a first visit opens): the picture is the whole screen. At the bottom, one block for each stage (its speed, its altitude, a ring of dots for its engines: white on, grey off, red crossed failed, drawn from the vehicle file's engine positions and the pose's per-engine state; a stage that has been let go has a body and a pose of its own, `others[]`), the mission clock between them with a line of events under it and a marker for each, and the event that has just happened said above the clock for six seconds (LIFTOFF, MAX Q, BOOSTER SEPARATION, BOOSTER CAUGHT...). Nothing else: the docks, the top bar, the cameras and the transport are away until the pointer moves or a key is pressed, and go after three seconds (`body.cast.awake`; `?awake=1` keeps them, for a screenshot). Click a number for miles and miles an hour. The gyro (below) is in the corner and `G` hides it. The line of events is from the first event the page saw (section 14's limit: the page derives them from the poses and a flight sought past an event does not list it); for a recording its length is the recording's, for a live flight it is ten minutes, growing. Narrow windows (under 900 px) put the clock and the line above the two blocks and drop the engine rings and the gyro.

**The director** (`director.js`, the **Auto** button on the camera bar, key `A`, and on at a first visit to the Broadcast): a table of the flight's kinds of moment, from the pose alone: the pad and the first 3 km, the ground camera; the climb to staging, the tracking camera; 12 s after a stage is let go, a chase view; the stage coming back, in turns a chase view of it and of the ship and, in its last 2.5 km or held in the arms, the ground camera; the rest, a chase view in the air and the Earth view above 120 km. A shot is held for 6 s so that it does not flicker at a threshold; a cut dips the picture for a third of a second (`#v-cut`); a drag or a click on a camera takes the director off. It changes which body is followed (`data.focus`) without clearing the events. The rules are hand-written for this mission's kind of flight and were checked on this one flight (the selftest has the table's cases).

**The gyro** (`gyro.js`): the vehicle's attitude as a three-gimbal gyroscope in the corner of the picture, and three rate gauges in the Attitude lens. The attitude in the local horizon's frame (x across the range, y up, z downrange: `World.localQ`, the launch frame carried along by the planet's turn and the vehicle's travel) is split into three turns, `q = R_y(lean) R_x(tilt) R_y(roll)`: the outer ring turns about the vertical by the direction the vehicle leans toward, the middle ring about the horizontal axis it is hinged on by how far it leans, the inner ring and the vehicle about the vehicle's own axis. The vehicle in the inner ring is the outline of what is still on it; a red stripe shows its roll. **Why this split and not heading, pitch and roll**: a launcher stands on its tail, where heading and roll are the same axis (the gimbal lock of an aircraft's angles, which are not defined for a vertical axis), while a lean, a tilt and a turn about the axis are; at the pad the tilt is zero and the lean is kept from before, so that nothing jumps when the vehicle starts to tilt, and the lean and the roll together are the vehicle's turn about the vertical. The tilt is from the *local* vertical: the simulator's `tilt` is from the launch vertical, and the two differ by the angle the planet's curve gives over the range (3.6 degrees at 400 km), which the selftest allows for. The rate gauges are the body's turn rates (about the axis, and across it) on a scale of 20 degrees a second. The instrument is drawn into the same canvas, after the world, with a viewport and a scissor of its own (`World.insets`), so it is in a screenshot; inside a dock it is clipped to what the dock shows (the card has no backdrop of its own, because the instrument is on the canvas behind it).

**The CFD view** (Aerodynamics lens, *CFD*): the camera is the new **Tunnel** one, fixed to the vehicle and looking along the normal of the section with the air coming from the left, nose to the left, as a CFD picture is drawn, so the angle of attack is the vehicle's angle to the air; a legend with numbers and a badge that says which flow is on the screen (*live solver, Mach 3.3, axisymmetric*, or *computed case, Mach 3, 4 degrees, 3D*); the cards of the plant's numbers and the charts are folded away in this view, and what is left is **what the flight flies** (below) and the solver's case. The two flows (the live solver on the graphics card and the computed 3D case) and each change of the case fade into one another over about a third of a second instead of jumping (`CfdView` keeps a layer for each case). **The map** (`cfdui.js` `AeroMap`) draws one coefficient (C_N, C_A or C_m) of the tables the flight flies on (`aero.cfd` in the vehicle file) over Mach and angle of attack with the same bilinear interpolation as the plant (`cfd_aero.hpp`), the nodes the solver was run at, the flight's path across it from the page's history and its place now with the table's value there; below the table's first Mach number it is hatched and says that the engineering build-up flies.
