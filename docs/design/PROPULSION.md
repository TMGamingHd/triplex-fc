# Propulsion: the engines, the nozzle, the gimbal, and an engine out (ADR-057)

> Status: **built on the host, tested against hand calculations** (accepted 18 Oct 2026, ADR-057): `sim/vehicle/vehicle6.hpp` (the plant), `sim/vehicle/propulsion.hpp` and `tools/sim/tfc_propulsion.cpp` (the analysis), `tests/test_propulsion.cpp`; the vehicle files are generated by `tools/vehicle/gen_starship_return.py`. Every number of a real engine here is from the public pages as I remember them, or my estimate, and is marked so. None is SpaceX data; nothing has run on a board.

## 1. What this adds, in one paragraph

Before this round every stage had **one** gimbal angle shared by all its engines that gimbal, a thrust that was the vacuum thrust less the ambient pressure on the exit area, a mass flow at a constant specific impulse, and roll was done by cold-gas thrusters. Now: each engine that gimbals has **its own angle and its own servo** (travel, rate, lag, second-order servo and play as the vehicle's `actuator` block says), the pitch and yaw command goes to every one of them and the **roll command is added along each engine's tangent** (the differential gimbal that rolls a real cluster), an engine can be weak, can have its gimbal **stick** or **run hard over** at a time, the specific impulse **falls with the throttle**, and a new tool, `tfc_propulsion`, analyses a vehicle's propulsion in closed form: the cluster, the thrust against altitude, each nozzle against an ideal one, the gimbal's authority at points of the burn, and what an engine out does to the balance of the cluster. The tests check the plant against hand calculations of the geometry, and the analysis against the plant.

## 2. The engines in the plant

For an engine `e` at throttle fraction `f` (the plant's `frac`, with the rise and tail time constants of `rise_s` and `tail_s`), in the ambient pressure `pa`:

```
thrust   T = max(0, f * thrust_scale * (thrust_vac * thrust_factor) - pa * exit_area)
mass flow  mdot = f * thrust_scale * (thrust_vac * thrust_factor) / (Isp(f) * g0)
Isp(f)   = isp_vac * (1 - isp_throttle_loss * (1 - f))            (isp_throttle_loss 0, the default: a constant isp_vac)
```

The vacuum thrust less the pressure on the exit area is the momentum thrust plus the pressure thrust of the nozzle, with the exit pressure taken as zero: it is exact in vacuum and low by `pe * Ae` in the air (for a nozzle that does not separate this is exact: `thrust = mdot ve + (pe - pa) Ae` and `thrust_vac = mdot ve + pe Ae`; and since `pe` scales with the throttle with the chamber pressure, so does the whole of `thrust_vac`, which is why the throttle multiplies it and the ambient term stays. It is wrong only where the flow separates, section 3). `thrust_factor` is the engine's own thrust relative to the rated (a weak engine is 0.97); `isp_throttle_loss` is the fraction of the specific impulse lost at zero throttle, linear in between.

**What is estimated.** The loss of specific impulse with throttle is **my estimate**: the files use 0.02, so an engine at 40 % throttle has 1.2 % less specific impulse and burns 1.2 % more propellant for its thrust; there is no public number. With the loss at zero, the 40-seed campaign gives 38 catches; with 0.02 it gives 37 (section 7): a landing is propellant-limited, and a per cent of it is one flight in forty.

## 3. The nozzle

`propulsion.hpp` has the isentropic relations of a calorically perfect gas (the formulas are in its header). With the chamber pressure `pc`, the area ratio `eps` and the ratio of specific heats `gamma` of an engine in the file (`chamber_pressure_pa`, `area_ratio`, `gamma`; the plant does not read them, the analysis does):

| Engine (as the files have it) | `pc` | `eps` | `gamma` | Exit Mach | Exit pressure | Ideal Cf (vacuum, sea level) | Ideal exit area | File's |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sea-level Raptor | 350 bar | 34 | 1.2 | 4.13 | 0.90 bar | 1.870, 1.772 | 1.34 m² | 1.33 m² (−1 %) |
| vacuum Raptor | 350 bar | 90 | 1.2 | 4.81 | 0.26 bar | 1.945, 1.685 | 3.57 m² | 3.57 m² |

(`pc` about 350 bar, the area ratios 34 and 90 and the exit sizes are from my memory of the public pages; `gamma` 1.2 is my estimate for a methane and oxygen exhaust.) The throat that gives each file's vacuum thrust at the ideal coefficient is 0.0395 m² (0.224 m across) for both, which is what two engines of one family should have.

**A finding the tool made.** The first files had 4.5 m² for the vacuum engine's exit (a 2.4 m exit, from memory). With an area ratio of 90 and the thrust of the file the ideal exit area is 3.57 m², 26 % smaller, and the sea-level thrust of the file was 0.831 of its vacuum thrust against 0.866 for the ideal nozzle: one of the four numbers (thrust, exit area, area ratio, chamber pressure) was wrong. I set the area to the ideal one. The engine is not lit in the atmosphere, so no flight moved by more than the pressure on a square metre at 45 km (150 N). The tool's check is now a test on the shipped file (`propulsion_the_shipped_starship_class_vehicle...`): every nozzle in it is within 5 % of an ideal one and its sea-level thrust loss within 0.03 of the ideal's.

**The separation floor.** The exit pressure falls with the throttle (`pe` is proportional to `pc` at a fixed area ratio), so an engine throttled down at sea level runs over-expanded. Below `pe / pa` of about 0.35 (the Summerfield criterion for a bell nozzle, from memory) the flow separates from the wall: the thrust becomes unsteady and the side load can exceed what the gimbal and the structure were sized for. For the sea-level engine of the files the floor is **39 % throttle, and the files' lowest throttle is 40 %**: the margin is one per cent, and a landing burn at sea level on one engine at its lowest throttle is right at it. The vacuum engine separates at sea level **even at full throttle** (its exit pressure is 0.26 bar against 1.01 bar): it must not be lit in the lower atmosphere, which the mission respects (the ship lights it at 45 km). The plant does **not** model the side load of a separated nozzle: it is only reported.

## 4. The gimbal

### 4.1 The model

With `per_engine_gimbal: true` in the vehicle file, every engine that has `gimbal: true` (the default for a main engine) has its own angles, `eg_p` and `eg_y` (degrees, the pitch plane and the yaw plane), and its own servo state. Each substep of the plant:

1. The command for the engine is the stage's pitch and yaw command, plus the roll command along the engine's tangent: with the engine at `(y, z)` and radius `r = hypot(y, z)`, a roll command `c_r` adds `roll_gain * c_r * z / r` to the pitch deflection and `roll_gain * c_r * y / r` to the yaw deflection. (A positive pitch deflection turns the thrust toward −Y, a positive yaw toward +Z, as the plant always had it; the tangent of a positive roll about +X at `(y, z)` is `(−z, y)`, so a positive roll command gives a positive roll torque. An engine on the axis, or with `roll_gain` 0, takes no part in roll.)
2. The engine's own **limits** apply: `gimbal_limit_deg`, `gimbal_rate_dps` and `gimbal_lag_s` of the engine if it has them, else the stage's, else the vehicle's. The servo is the first-order lag and rate limit the stage's gimbal always had, or the second-order servo with play of the `actuator` block when the file has one (`drive_servo`, the same routine).
3. A **fault** from the engine's `fault_time_s`: `"stuck"` leaves the angles where they were whatever the command, `"hardover"` sends the command to `fault_pitch_deg` and `fault_yaw_deg`, which the servo follows at the engine's rate.

The thrust of an engine is `T` times the unit vector `(cos dp cos dy, −sin dp cos dy, sin dy)` with `dp` and `dy` its angles plus its cant and the vehicle's misalignment; the force and the moment about the centre of gravity are summed engine by engine (`loads()`). The cosine loss, the lateral force and the torque of a deflection come out of that sum, with no separate formula. With every engine at the same angle the sum is the stage-wide gimbal of before to rounding (the first test, to a millionth of a newton metre); with `per_engine_gimbal` false nothing in the plant changed.

The design probes (`angular_accel_with`) put every engine at what a unit command asks of it, so the flight computers' gains are designed on the roll authority of the differential gimbal too.

### 4.2 What the tests check against a hand calculation (`tests/test_propulsion.cpp`)

* A deflection `d` of `n` engines on a ring at the aft end gives a torque `n T arm sin d` and an axial force `n T cos d` (the cosine loss), for 1°, 4° and 8°.
* An engine that does not gimbal, or has a travel of 2° when 6° is asked, gives the sum of its own terms, including the axial force at its offset (a ring is not balanced when its engines are at different angles): the per-engine plant and a hand sum agree to 1 part in 10⁵, and the shared-angle plant of before cannot tell the engines apart.
* The differential gimbal gives a roll torque `n T r sin(d)` within 0.5 %, none about the other two axes, the opposite for the opposite command, and nothing for an engine with no `roll_gain`.
* A weak engine (`thrust_factor` 0.9) at +Y adds `y ΔT` of pitch torque and removes `ΔT` of thrust, and the pitch gimbal that cancels it (the analysis computes it) leaves under 2 % of the torque; an engine at 0 is an engine out.
* A stuck gimbal stays at the angle it had, a hard-over one goes to its angle at its rate, and the others follow the command.
* The specific impulse at half throttle with a loss of 0.06 is 3 % lower and the mass flow 3 % higher.
* The roll authority the design probe sees is `Σ T r sin(1°) / I_x`.

## 5. The analysis: `tfc_propulsion VEHICLE.json [--strict] [--fill 1,0.5,0.05] [--detail]`

For each stage with engines: the cluster, the burn and the velocity it can give, the thrust and the specific impulse against altitude, each kind of engine's nozzle, the gimbal's servo, the authority at points of the burn, the engine out, and the lowest throttle. A finding is a line that starts `!`; `--strict` exits 3 on one. CI runs it on the mission vehicle (a report, with the shipped file at zero findings).

### 5.1 The booster and the ship, as the files have them

(From `build/rel/tfc_propulsion vehicles/missions/starship_return.json`, 18 Oct 2026; the burn figures are before gravity and drag.)

| | Booster (stage 0) | Ship (stage 1) |
|---|---|---|
| Engines | 33: 13 gimbal (3 at r = 0.95 m, 10 at 2.25 m), 20 fixed (at 3.85 m) | 6: all gimbal (3 sea-level at 0.95 m, 3 vacuum at 2.55 m) |
| Thrust, sea level / vacuum | 80.8 MN / 85.3 MN | 14.4 MN / 15.8 MN |
| Isp, sea level / vacuum | 331.7 s / 350.0 s | 330.4 s / 364.7 s |
| Burn at full throttle | 3,650 t in 147 s, 3,551 m/s | 1,600 t in 361 s, 9,072 m/s |
| Thrust-to-weight at the start | 1.46 (sea level) | 0.84 (sea level), 0.93 (vacuum) |
| Gimbal: travel, rate, lag | 8°, 15°/s, 0.08 s | 6°, 15°/s, 0.08 s |
| A sine of the full travel is followed up to | 0.30 Hz | 0.40 Hz |
| Pitch or yaw torque per degree, at the start / middle / end of the burn | 0.008 / 0.010 / 0.028 rad/s² | 0.032 / 0.042 / 0.128 rad/s² |
| … at the full travel | 0.066 / 0.078 / 0.220 rad/s² | 0.19 / 0.25 / 0.77 rad/s² |
| Roll by differential gimbal, per degree of the roll command | 0.019 / 0.027 / 0.045 rad/s² | 0.027 / 0.048 / 0.172 rad/s² |
| Roll by the cold-gas thrusters, at full | 0.0037 / 0.0053 / 0.0088 rad/s² | 0.0037 / 0.0067 / 0.024 rad/s² |
| Worst single engine out (sea-level thrust, start of the burn) | engine 28 (outer): loses 3.0 % of the thrust, leaves 9.4 MN m (the thrust acts 120 mm off the axis); trimmed with 0.30° = **4 %** of the travel; worst pair 7 % | engine 38 (a vacuum engine): loses 16.3 %, leaves 6.0 MN m (495 mm off the axis); trimmed with 1.69° = **28 %** of the travel; worst pair 43 % |
| Lowest thrust one engine can hold at sea level | 899 kN (the stage near empty weighs 21.5 MN) | 717 kN (a vacuum engine at its lowest throttle at sea level) |

What these say, plainly:

* **The booster's gimbal is weak but the vehicle is slow.** 0.066 rad/s² at the full travel is 3.8°/s²: it turns a 5,600 t stack as a heavy ship turns, and the servo cannot follow a sine of the full travel above 0.3 Hz. This is why the attitude loop of the booster has a low crossover and why `tfc_margins` finds its margins where they are (`CONTROL_LOOP.md` section 7).
* **The differential gimbal is five times the cold-gas thrusters in roll while the engines burn** (0.019 against 0.0037 rad/s² per degree of command at the start), and the thrusters are what is left in the coast. The roll loop's gains are designed from the probes, so they use both.
* **A single engine out is a small trim for the booster and a large one for the ship.** The booster's 33 engines make an engine out a 3 % loss and a trim of a few tenths of a degree. The ship has six, so one out is 16 % of the thrust and over a quarter of its travel to trim; two out are 43 %, and the ship still has travel to spare. (The analysis assumes the engines left are healthy and gimbal; a failed gimbal is the next section.)
* **The ship's roll loop was the weak loop of the mission** (6° of phase margin and 2.9 dB with the cold-gas thrusters alone, `CONTROL_LOOP.md` section 7): their authority is 0.0037 rad/s² at full command. **With the differential gimbal it is 65° and 18 dB in powered flight** (`tfc_margins`, 802 probes), because the design's gains no longer hit their cap. With the engines off, in the coast, the thrusters are all there is, and the roll loops there are 65° and 18 dB too (the gains fit the thrusters).

## 6. What flying it showed

Flown with `tfc_mission` on the three flight computers (release build; each a single flight unless said):

| Case | Ship | Booster |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline (per-engine gimbal, differential roll, Isp loss) | orbit 232 x 256 km | caught, 0.9 m from the site |
| **The booster's gimbal locked** (travel 0) | crashed, 7.1 km from the site | crashed, 7.2 km from the site, 96° from upright |
| One inner engine's gimbal **hard over to 3°** at T+30 s | orbit 234 x 258 km | caught, 1.3 m from the site |
| One outer (fixed) engine **out** at T+50 s | orbit 229 x 260 km | crashed, 507 m from the site |
| One inner (gimbal) engine **out** at T+50 s | orbit 228 x 260 km | crashed, 310 m from the site |
| Earth rotating (`starship_return_earth.json`) | orbit 229 x 260 km | caught, 1.1 m from the site |

* **The gimbal makes the difference between a flight and a crash**: lock it and the booster is lost on the way up. A single engine hard over at 3° is trimmed by the rest, which the analysis predicted (an engine's lateral force at 3° against the authority of the other twelve).
* **An engine out is survivable for the ship and not for the booster's return.** The ascent is trimmed and the ship reaches orbit in both cases. The booster arrives heavier (the stage burned less propellant for the same time) and the return fails in the last few hundred metres: it loses its attitude at about 400 m (a crossrange of 500 m grows to 970 m in 6 s, 170° from upright at the arms' height). I tried bounding the landing burn's planned deceleration by what the engines can give at the mass the vehicle has; it did not change the result and moved the baseline, so I removed it. The cause is the fragility of the landing that `FUTURE_WORK.md` 6a already records (0 of 100 flights under the assumed dispersions), not a missing trim. It is not fixed here.
* **An inner engine out is worse for the landing**: the booster lands on its three inner engines (groups 0, 1 and 2) and on the thirteen when it needs more, and the flight computers have no engine health, so with one of the three dead they still count three and ask for a thrust that is a third short. A real vehicle would choose which engines to light.
* **The loops' margins moved, and not only for the better** (`tfc_margins`): the ship's roll loop from separation to the entry went from 6° and 2.9 dB to **65° and 18 dB**; the booster's roll loops are 50° to 65°. The stack's roll loop at lift-off went from 10.7° and 5.1 dB to **7.9° and 2.1 dB**: the gains of the first point are designed for the engines at full thrust, and at the first probe the engines have not built their thrust up, so the loop starts on the thrusters alone with a gain too low. It moves no flight; scaling the roll command by the thrust, as the pitch and yaw command is, would be the cure and is not done.
* **The 40-seed campaign** (noise seeds, 15 s pad): 37 caught of 40 and 40 of 40 in orbit with the per-engine gimbal, the differential roll and the Isp loss. Without the Isp loss: 38 of 40. With the loss and the stage's shared angle (the plant of before): 37 of 40. The per-engine gimbal does not cost a catch; the loss of specific impulse, which is a per cent more propellant at the landing, costs one flight in forty at the margin the landing has.

## 8. What is not modelled

* **The gimbal actuator's load and power**: no torque limit, no hinge moment from the thrust, no current. The travel, rate, lag, second-order response and play are modelled; the force the actuator must make is not.
* **The moving mass of the engine** (its inertia turns with the gimbal and reacts on the vehicle): the thrust moves, the mass does not.
* **The side load of a separated nozzle, and the unsteady thrust of the start and of throttling**: reported by the analysis (section 3), not in the plant.
* **The feed and the pressurisation**: no turbopump transient, no tank pressure or ullage, no cavitation, no propellant stratification; the mass flow is thrust over specific impulse, and the start is a first-order rise.
* **A differential throttle** as a control (the real booster's outer engines are fixed; the model does not throttle them against each other).
* **The mixture ratio and the propellant that is left unburnt**; the sloshing of the tanks and the bending of the structure are in the plant (`DYNAMICS.md`) and are unchanged.
* **Per-engine random dispersion** of thrust and angle in the Monte Carlo: the plant takes `thrust_factor` per engine, but `tfc_mission_mc` still disperses the vehicle's thrust as a whole (`thrust_scale`).
* **The numbers**: the nozzle data, the gimbal travel and rate of the files, the specific-impulse loss and the thrusters are from memory of public pages or my estimates; nothing here is a measurement of an engine.
