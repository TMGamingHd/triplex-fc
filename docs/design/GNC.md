# Guidance, navigation and control of a whole mission

> Status: **built on the host, not run on a board** (accepted 10 Oct 2026, ADR-039). The flight computers' code in `core/include/tfc/` (`dmath.hpp`, `nav.hpp`, `peg.hpp`, `attitude.hpp`, `descent.hpp`, `mission.hpp`, and the parts of `flight.hpp`, `estimator.hpp` and `protocol.hpp` that carry them), the plant it flies (`sim/vehicle/`), the design tool that makes its tables (`mission_design.hpp`) and the host tool that flies a vehicle file's mission (`tools/sim/tfc_mission.cpp`) are built, tested (section 10) and flown end to end on the host. **Nothing here has run on the STM32G474, nobody has measured how long the guidance takes on it (section 11 says what that leaves open), and the firmware does not yet receive GNSS frames or send the propulsion and surface frames.** The numbers in section 10 are measured; where a number is a design estimate it says so.

## 1. What it is, and what it is for

Until this change the flight computers did one thing: hold a pitch program. They were given the angle the vehicle should have against time, and they steered the gimbal to it. They did not know where the vehicle was, could not turn it through more than about 80 degrees, could not light or cut an engine, and had no notion of an orbit or a landing.

They now fly a **mission**: a table of phases (section 7) that says, for each stretch of a flight, what is being done and what ends it. The computers navigate (section 3), steer a burn to an orbit (section 4), hold any attitude through any angle (section 5), bring a stage back and land it (section 6), and command the engines, the events and the control surfaces as well as the gimbal (section 8). The mission is written in the vehicle file (section 9); a design run on the simulator turns it into the constant tables the computers carry.

Two missions are in the repository (`vehicles/missions/`):

* **`starship_return.json`**: a Starship V3 class stack. Lift-off, ascent on a pitch program, hot staging, **orbital insertion of the ship** into a 250 km circular orbit with PEG, and **the booster's return**: flip, boost-back, entry under three grid fins, landing burn, **caught by the tower's arms**. Only a vehicle with more than one stage has a stage to bring back.
* **`rocket_recovery.json`**: a small single-stage rocket. It has nothing to land with but a canopy, so its mission ends in a drogue and a main parachute: **a smaller rocket just comes back down.**

What it is not: the flight software of any real vehicle. The algorithms are textbook ones (strapdown navigation, a numerical predictor-corrector in the family of Powered Explicit Guidance, a quaternion PD loop, zero-effort-miss landing guidance) written for this project; the vehicle is a model built from public numbers and my estimates (`RECOVERY.md` section 2); every limit is in section 11.

## 2. Frames and conventions

* **Navigation frame.** Inertial, non-rotating, centred on the planet, **+X up through the launch point at T-zero, +Y downrange, +Z crossrange**. It is the frame the vehicle simulator integrates in and the one every target is written in. (A GNSS receiver reports in a planet-fixed frame; the simulator's receiver hands over the solution already in this frame: the one modelling shortcut in navigation.)
* **Body frame.** +X along the axis toward the nose; **pitch is a turn about +Z, yaw about +Y, roll about +X** (the planes the gimbal commands have always been named for).
* **The estimator's attitude** is the quaternion of the *sensor* frame relative to a *level* frame (gravity down, the heading it had when it aligned on the pad). The sensor's X, Y, Z are the body's Y, Z, X; the level frame's axes are the navigation frame's (Y, Z, X). `att::body_to_nav` and `att::estimator_quaternion` convert both ways, and `tests/test_attitude.cpp` checks them against a rotation made independently.
* **Arithmetic.** Everything in the guidance and navigation is `double`, built from `+ - * /` and `sqrt` only (`dmath.hpp`: sine, cosine, arctangent, exponential, logarithm, three-vectors, quaternions and 3x3 matrices, accurate to better than 1e-14 relative against the C library, `tests/test_dmath.cpp`), with loops of fixed length. Three replicas given the same frames compute the same bits (ADR-006); the library's transcendental functions are not required to agree between implementations, and a guidance law that integrates a trajectory would amplify the difference. No heap, no exceptions, no RTTI.

## 3. Navigation (`nav.hpp`)

**Strapdown.** The accelerometers' consensus (the three-way vote the computers already did) is the specific force in the sensor frame. The estimator's quaternion and the axis permutation of section 2 turn it into the navigation frame; adding gravity (a point mass with J2, `nav::Gravity`) gives the acceleration, integrated with **velocity Verlet** (second order, and it keeps the energy of a coasting orbit). On the pad the accelerometers read the pad's reaction and gravity cancels it, so a vehicle at rest stays where it is with no special case.

**GNSS aiding.** A fix (position and velocity, three CAN frames `0x506` to `0x508`, each component a signed 24-bit number; `protocol.hpp`) corrects the inertial solution by a fixed fraction of the difference: **35 % of the position difference and 30 % of the velocity difference** (`NavConfig::k_pos`, `k_vel`), an alpha-beta observer, the steady-state Kalman filter for white acceleration noise with a fix every tenth of a second. A fix more than 2 km or 60 m/s from the inertial solution is **gated out**; thirty in a row mean the inertial solution is the one that is wrong and the navigator restarts from the fix. With no fix the inertial solution coasts and the time since the last fix is reported.

**The attitude estimator in powered flight.** The estimator corrects its tilt from the accelerometer, which on the pad measures gravity. Under thrust it measures thrust and drag, and with a thrust-to-weight near one it accepts thrust as gravity and pulls the estimate toward the thrust direction (`EstimatorConfig::use_accel` says so, and `SIM_FIDELITY.md` section 3.2 measured it). A flight computer that flies a mission therefore **switches the accelerometer correction off whenever it is not on the pad** (`FlightFunction::step`), and the attitude is the gyros' integral. A staging was where this showed: with the correction on, the estimated attitude of the ship was pulled 15 to 46 degrees away by the change of thrust at hot staging while the vehicle itself had not moved.

**Attitude aided by the GNSS velocity.** Gyros alone drift; five minutes of a booster's return left a tilt error of 1 to 1.5 degrees, and the catch's pins are 32 m from the centre of gravity (0.56 m for each degree). Under thrust the inertial solution turns the specific force by the attitude error, and the error integrates into velocity, so the part of the GNSS velocity innovation that is **across the specific force** is the attitude error. At each fix, with `f` the specific force in the navigation frame, `dv` the velocity innovation and `T` the time since the last fix, the navigator forms `k_att k_vel (f x dv) / (|f|^2 T)` (`k_att` = 0.02, only above `f_att_min` = 8 m/s^2) and the flight function turns the estimator's quaternion by it (`AttitudeEstimator::rotate_level`). The derivation, with the fix-to-fix recursion `e = (1 - k_vel)(e + delta)`, is in the comment at `Navigator::apply_fix`. It leaves the rotation about the specific force (the roll of a vehicle thrusting along its axis) unobserved, as it must. Measured (section 10): the estimated attitude is within 0.04 to 0.3 degrees of the truth through the whole Starship mission.

**What it is not**: a Kalman filter with a covariance, an estimator of the accelerometers' biases and scale errors, a model of the planet's rotation inside the navigator, or a second receiver. A single GNSS receiver's fix goes to all three computers (`GnssModel` in `avionics.hpp`), so a bad fix is a common-mode fault the redundancy cannot catch; the gate is the only defence (section 11).

## 4. Powered ascent and orbital insertion (`peg.hpp`)

A numerical predictor-corrector in the family of **Powered Explicit Guidance**. The vehicle burns at the thrust acceleration `a(t) = c / (tau - t)` of a constant mass flow (`c` the exhaust speed, `tau = m / mdot`) and steers by a pitch angle and a yaw angle that change **linearly with time** from the local horizontal in the plane of the target orbit. It cuts the engines after `T` seconds. The five numbers `(theta0, theta1, psi0, psi1, T)` are the unknowns. The **predictor** integrates the burn from the present state under real gravity (velocity Verlet, 12 steps) to its end; the **corrector** asks five things of that end state: the target radius, speed and flight-path angle, and zero position and velocity out of the plane. It solves them by Newton's method with Levenberg-Marquardt damping and a Jacobian from finite differences of the predictor, warm-started from the last solution shifted forward in time. The position along the track is free: where the burn ends is whatever the other five say.

Why not the classical closed-form integrals: they are exact for a small steering angle and a short burn, and an upper stage that burns most of its propellant while its thrust turns through 60 degrees leaves their linearisation; the iteration on them **diverged on exactly the Starship ship's case** (the test keeps it, `tests/test_peg.cpp`). The numerical form has no such limit and costs more: a cycle is seven predictors of twelve steps, which `Peg::work` spreads one predictor per call so that the cost in any one frame is bounded (a cycle is the base prediction, five columns of the Jacobian and a trial step: seven; the Cortex-M4F cost is **not measured**).

**The design's solution is the start.** The design run solves the burn from scratch once (thousands of predictions at once, in `Mission::set_design_mode`, never on in flight) and records the five numbers; they are the PEG phase's seed in the table (`p[3..7]`), so the computers only track a solution.

**The engines do not stop when they are told to.** A Raptor decays with a time constant of 0.8 s after the command, which at the ship's final acceleration (about 7 g) adds roughly 54 m/s after the cut-off: the first ship flown in the closed loop ended in a 250 x 436 km orbit instead of a circular one. The PEG target speed is reduced by `a * tail` (`p[9]`, the mean `tail_s` of the engines in the phase's groups; `a` the thrust acceleration at the time). The same lead is in the boost-back's cut-off (section 6).

Targets are written as a circular altitude, or an apogee and perigee with the altitude the burn ends at, or a radius, speed and flight-path angle (`PhaseSpec`); `orbit_point` turns the ellipse into the radius, speed and angle at the cut-off.

## 5. Attitude control through any angle (`attitude.hpp`, `flight.hpp`)

**The error is a rotation.** `controller.hpp`'s two tilt angles describe a vehicle within about 80 degrees of the vertical; a booster that flips to fly engines first, or a ship that goes from a vertical ascent to a belly-first entry, is outside it. The attitude is a unit quaternion `q` (body to navigation); the error quaternion `conj(q) q_ref` is the rotation, in body axes, that brings the vehicle to the reference, and its rotation vector (the short way round) is the error on the three body axes.

**The law** is a PD with an integral on each axis, `u = kp e + kd (w_ref - w) + integral`, with anti-windup (no integrating while saturated with the error still pushing the same way), the output slew-limited (0.6 degrees a frame) and limited (8 degrees of demand). **Gains are in degrees of effector per degree of error and per degree/second of rate error**, and the reference rate is fed forward (a ramp reference has no steady lag). The output of each axis is in degrees of the effector for that axis: the gimbal's angle, a surface's deflection, a thruster's duty. `Limits` and the gains are tables, not constants.

**The reference.** The sequencer (section 7) gives an attitude target each frame; the reference **turns toward it at the phase's own rate** (`slew_dps`), so a flip is a manoeuvre and a change of phase is not a step. The rate has to be what the effectors can follow: the booster's flip is flown on cold-gas thrusters that give 0.026 rad/s^2 at full demand, and a reference turning at 9 degrees a second made it lag, saturate and overshoot to 18 degrees a second; at 4.5 degrees a second it follows.

**The mixer** turns the three demands into commands. A phase names a mixer: which of the gimbal, the roll thrusters and the four surface channels it uses. The mixer's gimbal pitch and yaw factors are 0 or 1; the roll thrusters take the roll demand. For the surfaces see below.

**Surfaces: an allocation that follows the flight.** The grid fins and flaps are shared among the three axes by a minimum-effort pseudo-inverse of their effect `B` (the angular acceleration per degree of each surface, measured on the plant by the design run, section 9): `u = B^T (B B^T + eps I)^-1 e`. Two things were learned the hard way. (1) The effect of a fin changes by an order of magnitude through an entry, and **its sign relative to the others changes with Mach and angle of attack**; one allocation for a whole phase (the average of the effects) made the designed roll and yaw effectiveness pass through zero and change sign, the designed gains went negative and saturated, and the booster rolled up to 3.6 rad/s. The allocation is therefore **part of the gain schedule** (`GainTrack::alloc`, interpolated like the gains), made at each design point. (2) The demand is limited to 8 "degrees"; if a unit of demand were one radian per second squared the fins would never deflect more than 0.6 degrees. The allocation is scaled so that **the full demand takes the most loaded surface to 80 % of its travel**.

**Gains from the plant.** For an axis with effectiveness `b` (angular acceleration per unit demand, from the probes) and aerodynamic stiffness `a` (the change of angular acceleration per radian of turn, positive for an unstable vehicle): `kp = (wn^2 + max(a, 0)) / b`, `kd = 2 zeta wn / b`, `ki = ki_over_kp kp` (the same as `design.hpp` for the pitch program), **converted from degrees of effector per radian to degrees per degree** (a factor of pi/180: the loop takes its error in degrees; a design that forgot it had gains 57 times too high and chattered the gimbal at its limits), and clamped at `kp_max`. The bandwidth `wn` is 0.5 rad/s by default and a phase may name its own (the landing burn uses 1.0, because tilting through 40 degrees in a few seconds at the start of the burn was too much for 0.5).

**The gimbal turns the vehicle in proportion to the thrust.** The gains of a phase were designed at the thrust the design flight had, and a landing burn runs on 3 engines and then 13. Each point of the schedule carries the thrust it was designed at (`GainTrack::thrust`); the demand is scaled by `thrust_design / thrust_now` (clamped to 0.1 to 10) on its way to the gimbal, and the demand's own limit is scaled by the inverse so that the gimbal can still reach its limit.

**Roll.** The roll thrusters act on the roll demand; engines off the axis (one or two of the inner three) turn a gimbal command into a roll torque larger than the thrusters can hold, which sent a booster spinning in the landing burn. The landing burn therefore runs on the symmetric set of three, and on thirteen.

## 6. Bringing a stage back (`descent.hpp`, and the phases of `mission.hpp`)

Only a stage of a vehicle with more than one stage has something to return; the phases below belong to a stage's own plan (`stage0` in the file) and are flown by computers the stage has once it is let go (section 8).

**Flip.** A coast whose attitude hold is "against the horizontal velocity" (`retro_horizontal`), ending when the attitude is within 8 degrees of it.

**Boost-back.** Burn against the horizontal velocity until the **predicted impact point** reaches the catch point. The prediction is `descent_predict`: a numerical integration (1 s steps, point mass, an exponential atmosphere, drag from a ballistic coefficient) of the fall from the present state with the velocity the engines will have when they have died away (the tail lead of section 4, along the axis the engines really push along, which is the vehicle's, not the one that was asked for). The burn ends when the miss, projected on the direction it began in, is zero or negative (plus an aim `bias_m` beyond, a thrust lift `pitch_up_deg` above the horizontal, and a propellant reserve `reserve_mass_kg` it will not burn into).

**The air the prediction uses is measured.** `descent_predict` needs a ballistic coefficient, and the one first assumed (7,000 kg/m^2, a guess from the booster's mass over a drag area) is not the plant's: the cut-off it gave left the booster kilometres from where the prediction said it would come down, and the landing burn could not take up the difference. The design run (section 9) now flies the mission once, samples `drag = -f_aero . v / m` along the glide weighted by the dynamic pressure, fits `beta = sum(q^2) / sum(q a_drag)` and flies the mission again with that value in the tables (4,413 kg/m^2 for the booster, against the 7,000 assumed).

**Entry (glide).** Engines off, falling engines first under three grid fins, steering by lift: the miss from `descent_predict` across the path becomes a sideways acceleration `2 miss / t^2`, which the angle of attack must give; the lift per degree is the dynamic pressure times `lift_area_per_deg_m2` over the mass; the angle is limited (`alpha_max_deg`, **12 degrees**: the three fins cannot hold more once the dynamic pressure builds, the body's restoring moment is larger than theirs, and 30 degrees lost attitude control at 5 kPa). A flying-tail-first body that tilts its nose toward a side is pushed away from that side, so the tilt that produces lift toward the site is `-vel cos(alpha) - side sin(alpha)` (getting the sign wrong sent the booster away from the site).

**Landing burn (`PoweredDescent`).** The thrust acceleration is the one that stops the vehicle at the catch point. *Vertically*, a speed profile `v_ref(h) = min(sqrt(sink^2 + 2 a_plan h), sink + h / approach_s)` (constant planned deceleration, then a gentle end), the feed-forward being the profile's slope times the speed and a feedback on the difference of the speeds on top. (The first version held the planned deceleration as the feed-forward all the way down and **hovered** until the propellant was gone; the feed-forward must be the slope of the profile, not its starting value.) *Horizontally*, the zero-effort-miss/zero-effort-velocity law, `a = -6 e / t^2 - 4 v / t`, with a horizon `t` that is the time the vertical profile takes **but never less than `horizon_min_s` (8 s)**: a position loop faster than the attitude loop that has to tilt the vehicle for it oscillates (4 s did, and put the vehicle 25 degrees off in the last seconds). The tilt is limited from `tilt_max_deg` (40) high up to `tilt_final_deg` (2.5) in the last `final_height_m`, and the burn **aims `aim_below_m` (1.5 m) under the catch point** so the pins come down through the arms still moving down at about the sink speed (ending the phase at the catch point itself cut the engines half a second before the pins arrived and the free fall gave 3.7 m/s). *Engines*: the fewest of the options that can deliver the thrust asked for, held while the thrust is not well under what the next fewer could give.

**The catch height depends on the propellant left.** The arms take the pins, which are 32 m ahead of the centre of gravity at the 25 t the mission assumed; with 60 t left the centre of gravity is lower, the pins end up about a metre too high and the vehicle hovers over the arms. The table carries `landing_height`, its `slope` per kg and the mass it was made at (`Mission::catch_height`), from `Vehicle6::stage_cg_from_aft` at two amounts of propellant.

## 7. The mission sequencer (`mission.hpp`)

A table-driven machine of **phases**. Each says what is being done (`kind`), which engine groups run, which events are asserted, which mixer and gains the attitude loop uses, how fast the reference may turn, and what ends it. It moves to the next phase when that happens and never moves back; the last phase stays.

| Kind | Does | Parameters (`p[]`) |
|---|---|---|
| `hold` | nothing; engines off | |
| `program` | fly the pitch program of the tables; the throttle from a track or a constant | |
| `peg` | steer a burn to a target with PEG | `p[0..2]` target radius, speed, flight-path angle; `p[3..7]` the design's seed; `p[8]` burnout mass; `p[9]` tail lead |
| `coast` | no engines (or the hot stage's, if groups are named); an attitude hold | `p[0..2]` the direction of a `fixed` hold |
| `boostback` | burn back toward the site | `p[0]` bias, `p[1]` reserve mass, `p[2]` pitch-up, `p[3]` tail |
| `glide` | fall engines first, steer by lift | `p[0]` alpha max, `p[1]` gain, `p[2]` steady angle, `p[3]` lift area per degree, `p[4..5]` gate speed and height, `p[6]` brake maximum |
| `landing` | the landing burn | `p[0..3]` the engine groups of each option, fewest first |
| `chute` | a parachute descent: events at altitudes, no attitude control | `p[0]`, `p[1]` the drogue's and main's altitudes |
| `done` | engines off | |

**Ends**: never; after a time; at a speed; at the apoapsis (after at least a time); descending through or climbing through an altitude; when the guidance says the burn is over (`cutoff`); at a mass; when the attitude is within so many degrees of the reference (and at least a second in the phase); at the landing burn's ignition (the height is under a value *and* has come down to the stopping height at the planned deceleration, times the margin); at the catch point (`touchdown`).

**Holds**: inertial (the reference the previous phase ended with), prograde, retrograde, radial, a fixed direction, against or along the horizontal velocity.

The machine holds only the phase, the time in it, the mass estimate (from the commanded flow, so a stage that has been let go starts from the mass the design recorded) and the state of the guidance in use, so a replica that restarts can take them from its peers (`Mission::State`).

## 8. The closed loop: three computers around a body (`avionics.hpp`, `mission_world.hpp`)

`Avionics` is what flies a body without a socket: three `FlightFunction`s (the same class the firmware runs), each with its own IMU model, the real ACT logic voting their gimbal commands, a shared GNSS receiver (`GnssModel`: Gaussian position and velocity errors, a rate, outages), and the consumer's vote of the rest: **the middle value of three** for the throttle, the roll and the four surface deflections, **two of three for each bit** of the engine groups and the events (`mid3`, `majority3` in `protocol.hpp`). The new CAN frames (`protocol.hpp`, `PROTOCOL.md`): `0x210 + node` the propulsion command (throttle 0.5 % a count, group mask, events, phase, roll 0.25 degree a count), `0x220 + node` the four surface deflections (12 bits each, 0.05 degree a count), `0x506` to `0x508` the GNSS fix. **In this host loop the propulsion and surface commands are passed between the functions and the vote in memory, not through the frames**, so the frames' quantisation is not in the measured results and the firmware does not yet send or receive them.

`MissionWorld` steps every body of a mission one frame at a time: the stack, and the stages it lets go. A stage let go is flown on as a vehicle of its own (`Vehicle6::spawn`, with the momentum and the centre of gravity of the whole conserved, `Detached`), and, if the mission gives it a plan (`stage0`...), with computers of its own that start with the attitude and the navigation state of the moment of separation and the mass the design recorded. Two ways to fly it: **closed loop** (the sensors, the vote, the loop) and **ideal** (the attitude of every body forced to the one its mission asks for, the engines and events as commanded; the design run, and a check of the guidance and the plant without the loop).

## 9. The mission in the vehicle file, and its design

The `mission` block of a vehicle file (`VEHICLE_SPEC.md` section 9) holds: the **site** (the tower's offset from the launch point, the arms' height, the capture radius), the **landing** settings, the **air** the descent predictor starts with, the attitude **limits**, the **design** settings (bandwidth, damping, ...), the **mixers**, and the **plans**: `main` for the computers that fly the whole vehicle and `stage0`, `stage1`... for the computers of a stage that has been let go. `mission_build.hpp` turns it into the constant `Tables` (the thrust and mass flow of the engines each phase runs, the targets worked out from the orbit asked for, the landing site, the catch height); `spec_io.hpp` reads and writes it (the written file reads back as the same vehicle, `tests/test_mission_sim.cpp`).

**The design run** (`design_mission`) flies the mission once in the ideal mode. Along it, every `sample_s` seconds, it **probes the plant**: it copies the vehicle and differences the angular acceleration for half a degree of each effector (the gimbal in the pitch plane and in the yaw plane, the roll thrusters, each surface channel) and for half a degree of attitude about each axis (the aerodynamic stiffness `a`). From the probes come, for every phase: the gains and the allocation of every point of the schedule, thinned to the 16 points a track holds; the thrust each was made at; from the flight itself the PEG seeds, the mass each stage had when it was let go, and (if a stage glides) the drag of the way down. It flies the mission **twice** if anything glides: the first measures the drag, the second is the flight the rest is taken from.

## 10. Verification, and what was measured

**Tests** (`tests/`, built with the address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers; the flight code's coverage gate is 100 % of lines and 98 % of branches, the simulator's 99 % and 90 %): `test_dmath.cpp` (every function against the library), `test_nav.cpp`, `test_peg.cpp` (the stiff ship case, an ellipse, the slicing, the limits), `test_attitude.cpp`, `test_descent.cpp` (the impact point against a brute-force integration, the predictor with and without air, the landing law flown in a point-mass model from four starts), `test_mission.cpp` (the tracks, every kind of phase and end and hold, an ascent to a circular orbit seeded and not, a booster's boost-back, entry and landing flown in a point-mass model to the catch point), `test_newtonian.cpp` and `test_landing_plant.cpp` (the plant: `AERODYNAMICS.md` sections 7 to 9, `RECOVERY.md` section 5), `test_mission_sim.cpp` (the file, the tables, the design, a rocket flown ideally and on its computers down to its parachutes, a two-stage vehicle whose lower stage is flown on by computers of its own).

**End to end** (`build/rel/tfc_mission vehicles/missions/starship_return.json --expect-catch --expect-orbit`, about 6 s on a release build; CI runs it): the Starship V3 class mission of this repository, designed and then flown on the three computers with a 2 m GNSS receiver:

| | Closed loop | Ideal |
|---|---|---|
| Ship's orbit | **234.1 x 257.2 km**, 7,753 m/s | 229.2 x 255.3 km |
| Booster | **caught by the tower**, centre of gravity 2.4 m from the site (the pins within the 1.5 m capture), 334,521 kg left (59.5 t of propellant) | caught, 0.6 m |
| Noise seeds 1 to 12 | 11 caught, 1 crashed 5.4 m off | |
| GNSS accuracy 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 3 m | all caught | |
| GNSS accuracy 4 m and 6 m | crashed 2.2 and 2.5 m from the site | |

The ship's orbit is circular to within about 5 % of the altitude, not exact: the PEG cut-off lead is the engines' decay time times the acceleration, and the decay is not exactly exponential in the plant. The catch is not robust: it needs a GNSS receiver of 3 m or better (a real tower would use differential corrections for the final approach; the model has none) and one seed in twelve missed. `rocket_recovery.json` lands under its parachutes in both modes.

**Wall time**: the whole Starship mission, design included, takes about 6 s in a release build (51 engines, 5,660 t at lift-off); under the sanitizers it is too long for the unit tests, which is why `test_mission_sim.cpp` flies the small vehicles.

## 11. Limits, in plain words

* **Not run on the target.** No timing of the guidance on the Cortex-M4F has been made. A PEG cycle is seven predictors of twelve steps in `double` (software double precision on a single-precision FPU), the glide's predictor is 600 steps every half second and the boost-back's 900 steps every tenth of a second: the cost per frame is bounded by construction (the cycle is spread out) but the predictors are not, and they may not fit the 10 ms frame. This is the first thing to measure on the board.
* **The firmware does not fly a mission yet.** `flight.hpp` has `enable_gnc`, `on_gnss_frame` and `step_gnc`; `firmware/app/src/main.cpp` does not call them, does not receive `0x506` to `0x508`, does not send `0x210` or `0x220`, and the tables are not generated into the image (`tfc_gen_tables` has no mission path). `tfc_simd`, the rig builder, the console's decoders and the viewer's multi-body pose are not extended either. The mission is flown by `tfc_mission` and the tests, on the host.
* **One GNSS receiver** feeds all three computers; a bad fix is common-mode.
* **The ship stops at orbit.** Deorbit, entry and landing of the ship are not flown (the plant has flaps and a belly-first aerodynamics, `AERODYNAMICS.md` sections 8 and 9, but there is no entry phase for the ship in the mission).
* **The catch is marginal** (section 10) and was tuned on one vehicle: the bias, the glide's angle, the landing deceleration, the time horizon and the bandwidths are the values that worked for it. Another vehicle needs its own, and the design does not find them.
* **Thrust-vector and surface models** are the plant's (a first-order lag, a rate limit); nothing here is a model of a specific actuator.
* The plant's grid fins, flaps and aerodynamics at hypersonic speed are engineering estimates (Newtonian impact theory, flat-plate and grid-fin formulas), not wind-tunnel or CFD data.
