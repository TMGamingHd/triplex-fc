## The decisions

## ADR-001: C++17 subset for flight logic
**Decision:** Portable `core/` in C++17, header-only, no heap, no exceptions, no RTTI, warnings as errors.
**Why:** SpaceX's public Starship software postings ask for C++, Rust or another systems language and real-time embedded experience; public writing about its flight software describes C and C++. A restricted C++ subset is the industry pattern for flight code (MISRA/JSF-style rules). **Alternatives:** C (simpler to certify, less expressive), Rust (attractive, but a weaker toolchain fit for Zephyr today). **Revisit:** Rust for a non-critical tool.

## ADR-002: Zephyr RTOS on the target
**Decision:** Zephyr, with the portable core tested on `native_sim`.
**Why:** Modern, upstream, actively maintained, first-class support for the Nucleo-G474RE (FDCAN, SPI, PWM, watchdogs) and an ISM330DHCX sensor binding, plus real space-industry interest (for example NASA cFS being ported to Zephyr by a space-electronics company). Its `native_sim` target lets the same firmware run on a PC for CI. **Honest note:** SpaceX's own stack is not public and what has been described publicly is dated (Linux and VxWorks have been mentioned). No one will hire on RTOS brand; what transfers is deterministic scheduling, priority discipline, timing measurement and testing. **Alternatives:** FreeRTOS (ubiquitous, lighter), bare metal (max determinism, fewer marketable skills). **Fallback:** if Zephyr bring-up stalls beyond milestone M1 by 2 weeks, drop to FreeRTOS; `core/` is unaffected.

## ADR-003: Time-triggered schedule at 100 Hz
**Why:** Predictable, measurable, easy to reason about for voting, and it mirrors how safety-critical buses are scheduled. **Alternative:** event-driven; simpler but jitter is harder to bound.

## ADR-004: Classic CAN 1 Mbit/s for v1
**Why:** The USB-CAN adapter's FD support with default firmware is unconfirmed; 8-byte frames fit our messages. CAN FD is a stretch goal that does not change `core/`.

## ADR-005: Real IMUs on a servo motion platform driven by a simulator
**Why:** Gives real sensor noise and latency in the loop (strong hardware-in-the-loop story) while keeping vehicle physics repeatable. **Cost:** servo bandwidth limits platform rates, so the simulated vehicle is time-scaled. **Alternative:** pure PC-injected sensor data (easier, weaker as a portfolio piece). Keep the injection mode as well, since CI needs it.

## ADR-006: Deterministic identical replicas with an estimator-state digest
**Why:** Bit-identical replicas make exact cross-checking possible and make silent divergence detectable. **Limit:** no protection against a shared software bug (see limitations).

## ADR-007: A damaged or lost frame consumes a sequence number
> **Superseded in mechanism by ADR-018** (frames carry SYNC's frame number and the receiver keeps a 32-cycle arrival history instead of a contiguous-counter tracker). The *requirement* it set still holds and is tested the same way: one lost, damaged or late frame costs exactly one sample, two isolated ones never isolate a healthy node.
**Decision:** `tfc::SeqTracker` advances its expected sequence number when a frame arrives with a bad CRC (`note_damaged`) and when a slot passes with nothing at all (`note_missing`). One corrupted or lost frame therefore costs one bad sample.
**Why:** Found with the virtual peers. Without this a single corrupted frame was followed by a false "sequence gap" on the next good frame, and a single lost frame likewise: two bad samples per event, so two isolated events inside the 3-of-5 window latched a healthy node, contradicting TFC-FDIR-004. The first half (corrupt) was fixed in PR #5; the audit before PR 1 showed the lost-frame half still cost two (two lost frames three apart latched a healthy node).
**Assumption:** the sender's counter advances with the schedule whether or not the frame reaches us. True for bus loss and for a node that hangs and recovers with its counter running (tested); a node that restarts its counter is reported once, which is the right outcome.
**Late frames (added after PR #8 merged, found by CI):** `note_missing()` alone broke the common jitter case. A peer frame that misses the 7 ms vote is marked missing and the expectation advances; the frame then arrives in the *next* frame carrying the number we had just skipped, which looked like a repeat, so a late frame cost two bad samples again. It showed up as `seq=2` on a healthy live run on the shared CI runner. The tracker now remembers how many slots it merely *assumed* missing (`pending_`, capped at 32) and accepts a frame carrying one of those numbers once, without moving the expectation. A burst is covered too: a stalled sender flushing several late frames at once (reproduced under CPU contention: `seq=2`; six repeats after the generalisation: `seq=0` with up to 8 `missing`). A genuine repeat, a number older than the assumed ones, or a frame after a damaged one is still an error. Regression tests were written first and failed before the fix.
**Alternatives:** weight sequence errors less than other bad samples (hides real sequence faults); drop sequence checking (loses the cheapest duplicate/replay detection). **Revisit:** if a sender is ever allowed to skip transmissions without advancing its counter, or if real hardware shows reordering (the late-arrival rule assumes a sender's frames stay in order).

## ADR-008: Duplex disagreement: attribute by continuity, otherwise hold the last good value and request Safe (sticky)
**Decision:** With two voting nodes that disagree, `RedundancyManager` (1) blames a node only if it jumped more than `duplex_arbitration_factor` (2.0) x tolerance from the last agreed value, set by the *previous* frame, while the other stayed within one tolerance; the output follows the consistent node. (2) Otherwise nobody is blamed, the output holds the last good value, and if unresolved disagreements persist (3 of the last 5 frames) a Safe request is raised that stays raised until `clear_safe_request()`. (3) Whenever any vote cannot produce a trustworthy value (unresolved duplex, no majority, no data) the output holds the last good value (`FrameReport::output`, `held_mask`).
**Why:** The audit found that in Duplex three single spikes inside five frames latched *both* survivors and dropped the system to Safe, while the same spikes in Triplex isolated only the culprit; and `vote3` returned `0.0` on a duplex miscompare with nothing holding the last command (FDIR-008 was only partly met). A step fault or a spike is easy to attribute: one node left the last agreed value, the other did not. A slow drift or a step barely above tolerance is not (both nodes are within reach of the reference), and a digest mismatch has no continuity at all: those must not be guessed, so they hold and request Safe, which is what FDIR-008 says.
**Research:** safety-critical systems keep the human or ground in the loop for anything the system cannot attribute. The Space Shuttle's computers voted a failed unit out and did not readmit it automatically; in the ALT-1 flight the crew manually isolated the failed computer and the vehicle landed on the remaining three (NASA write-up, klabs.org). Hence the sticky request that only an operator command lifts.
**Bug found while implementing, and the rule it produced:** the first version let the held (frozen) reference keep arbitrating. A replay with a 1.5 dps step on one survivor showed Safe requested at frame 202 and then the **healthy** node blamed at frame 229, because the vehicle had moved away from the stale value and the faulty node happened to sit near it. Arbitration now requires a *fresh* reference (previous frame trusted); a stale one never blames anyone. Cost: after an unresolved disagreement the system stays Safe even if one node later looks clearly wrong. That is the conservative outcome and is covered by tests.
**Alternatives rejected:** blame both (old behaviour: a transient in Duplex ends the mission); latest value wins / average (silently averages a faulty value, FDIR-008 forbids it); pick the node closest to the previous output with no margin (misattributes whenever the vehicle moves faster than the margin; the margins above are config and must be tuned per channel against real dynamics). **Revisit:** analytical redundancy (cross-sensor plausibility) could resolve drifts; that is later work.
**Amended by ADR-017 (and again for E19, below):** the reference the nodes are compared with is the last agreed value *carried forward by the last agreed step*, not the bare last value. Comparing with a standstill blamed the healthy node whenever the signal moved more than two tolerances per frame (the command channels), which the fault campaign found.

## ADR-009: Out-of-schedule traffic is counted by a catch-all filter and raises a rate alarm, not a latch
**Decision:** FC-A adds a catch-all CAN receive filter (second queue) whose frames are offered to the manager; frames on IDs outside the schedule are counted, and `bus_alarm_per_frame` (3) or more in one 10 ms frame raises `FrameReport::bus_alarm` for as long as it continues. FC-A prints `BUS ALARM RAISED/cleared`. No node is latched for it.
**Why:** The audit found babbling completely invisible in the live firmware: 2,352 stray frames on the bus and not a counter or alarm, because the acceptance filters (correctly, for the schedule slots) dropped them before the manager's `out_of_schedule` counter could see them, so TFC-FDIR-009's detector existed only in the replay tool. CAN frames carry an ID, not a sender, so a flood on unscheduled IDs cannot be attributed to a node; attributing it would be a guess. The response that is both honest and useful is the alarm (and, in the real system, the actuator node ignoring those IDs).
**Cost / revisit:** a catch-all filter makes every frame an interrupt on the target. At bring-up evaluate the cheaper hardware route (strict acceptance filters plus the FDCAN controller's lost-message and protocol-status counters, bus-load measurement on the logic analyzer). The real harm of a babbler (arbitration starvation) is a timing effect only hardware shows (F11, TFC-FDIR-009 stays an M requirement).

## ADR-010: Node life cycle: dwell, shadow-vote probation, strikes, and disabling
**Decision:** A node is in one of four states: `Healthy` (voting), `Latched` (excluded), `Probation` (excluded, being compared) or `Disabled` (excluded for the run). Latching counts a strike. A latched node first serves a **minimum dwell** (200 frames, 2 s). It then goes on **probation** when asked: by an operator command (policy `Manual`, the default), or automatically (policy `AutoTransient`, opt-in) only for a first latch whose cause looks transient (missing/damaged frames or a vote episode), never for a stuck sensor or digest mismatch and never for a repeat offender. On probation the node is still out of the vote, but every frame its data is compared with the **voted output of the healthy nodes** on all 8 channels and with their digest (a *shadow vote*). It must agree for 100 consecutive frames (300 after a repeat latch) to be readmitted; one bad or disagreeing frame sends it back to `Latched` (dwell restarts, new request needed, no new strike: it was never readmitted). **One node is on probation at a time.** A frame with no trustworthy reference (nobody healthy, Safe requested, the healthy nodes disagree) neither counts nor fails. **Strikes:** the 3rd latch of a node disables it (`max_strikes`); the 2nd when the cause is physical (`physical_causes`, default a stuck sensor or an intermittent fault, ADR-013). Strikes are counted over the whole run by default (`strike_window_frames` = 0), because a flight is minutes long. A `Disabled` node refuses reintegration; only the maintenance command `ClearDisabled` returns it to `Latched` with its strikes cleared. Safe stays sticky (ADR-008).
**Why (measured before this change):** the old `request_reintegration()` counted 100 *clean frames*, but a latched node was never compared with the vote, so "clean" only meant its frames arrived intact. A node still biased by 3 dps was readmitted at frame 120 (mode jumped back to Triplex), re-latched at 123, readmitted again at 230 and finally made permanent at 233. In Triplex the median hides that, but in Duplex a still-faulty node pulled next to a good one forces an unattributable disagreement (ADR-008). Now the same node is refused on its first probation frame (frame 213 in the test), and the report names the reason.
**Research (what each source supports; how I read it):**
- *Space Shuttle:* a computer that failed to meet a sync point was voted out and was not readmitted automatically; in the ALT-1 flight the crew manually isolated the failed computer (switch to STANDBY, then HALT) and the vehicle landed on the remaining three (NASA write-up on klabs.org, read directly). Hence Manual by default. The rule that a computer receiving two or more failure votes flags itself and resets its own votes comes from a search summary of the Sklaroff redundancy-management paper (a scan I could not read); it is not implemented here (listed under open items).
- *Boeing signal selection (patent US 5,710,776, via a summarising reader, not verbatim):* faults latch; failed signals keep being monitored so a latch can clear if the signal recovers; the number of latch clears is limited. This is the shape of probation (keep watching the excluded node) plus bounded strikes.
- *TTP/C (search summary of the Kopetz chapter):* exclusion and reintegration are separate mechanisms; a failed node stays failed for at least two TDMA rounds; at most one node is reintegrated per round. We mirror these as the minimum dwell and one-probation-at-a-time.
- *alpha-count (Bondavalli et al., IEEE Trans. Computers 2000; search summaries):* a count-and-threshold mechanism (+1 per error, x K per good observation) separates transient from intermittent and permanent faults. Strikes are the coarse version; the alpha-count itself is the planned next step for intermittent faults the 3-of-5 window cannot see.
- *AUTOSAR DEM (search summary of the spec):* counter-based debouncing before a fault is confirmed and "healing/aging" counted in operation cycles before it is cleared. Probation is a healing counter.
- *SpaceX:* nothing public about reintegration. The public description (three computers, each a pair of processors that cross-check; a disagreeing pair does not send commands) is a different layer: a node that fails silent.
**Differences from the plan I gave first, and why:** (1) strikes counted over the whole run, not "3 within 30 s": a flight is minutes long, the window is still configurable; (2) the strike limit for physical causes is 2, as proposed. **A requirement mismatch found on the way:** TFC-FDIR-007 said a channel that latches *twice* becomes permanent while the code defaulted to three; the requirement now states the policy exactly (3, or 2 for physical causes).
**Amended (fast reintegration, E6/D):** the dwell is 50 frames (0.5 s) after a *first, transient-looking* latch (cause only missing/CRC/sequence/vote) and stays 200 frames for a digest mismatch, a stuck or intermittent node and every repeat latch. Reason: every frame a node is out is a frame the system runs with less redundancy (and in Duplex a second fault can only end in Safe, E6), while the node must still pass the full 100-frame probation, and the strike limits still stop a node that keeps failing. `min_dwell_frames_transient` in the configuration; the full-dwell mechanism is still tested with it set to 200.
**Alternatives rejected:** readmitting after N clean frames without the shadow comparison (the bug above); readmitting without a dwell (a node faulting every few seconds would cycle in and out of the vote); automatic readmission as the default (airliners and the Shuttle keep a human in the loop, and an automatic policy can mask a wearing-out part); making the physical-cause rule a special case in the voter (it belongs to the supervisor).
**Known limits / open items:** each flight computer decides alone, with no agreement on membership, so two computers can hold different views of a node (the digest and a heartbeat state exchange are the planned remedy); a node that finds *itself* voted out keeps judging (the Shuttle rule above); recovery from "all nodes out" had no reference and needed a restart (fixed: ADR-014); the timing constants (200/100/300 frames) are proposals to be tuned from hardware measurements.

## ADR-011: Operator actions are CAN "ground command" frames
> **Amended by ADR-019:** the 4 reserved bytes now carry an authentication tag, `seq` is a command counter with a replay window, dangerous operations need an ARM frame first, and `disable` has an interlock. The sentences below describe the original design; what changed and why is in ADR-019.
**Decision:** Frame ID `0x510`, payload `opcode | node | 4 reserved | seq | CRC-8`. Opcodes: `Reintegrate`, `Disable`, `ClearDisabled`, `ClearSafe`. The manager applies commands at the end of the frame in which they arrive and reports each outcome (`accepted`, `already done`, or `refused: <why>`) in `FrameReport::commands`; FC-A prints it. All operations are idempotent, so a repeated frame is harmless and there is no replay protection. Scripts (`--command FRAME:OP[:NODE]`) and `tfc_peers command` send the same frames.
**Why:** a frame can be scripted at an exact frame number, so the whole life cycle (fault, latch, command, probation, readmission) is a single reproducible command line, offline through `tfc_replay` and live against FC-A; every flight computer on the bus receives the same command at the same time; and it works unchanged over the USB-CAN adapter later. `0x510` has lower priority than every control frame, as an operator action should. **Rejected:** a Zephyr shell on the console (only reachable by typing into one process, not scriptable to a frame number, a different path on `native_sim` and hardware).
**Limits:** no authentication; any node on the bus could send one. That is acceptable for a bench rig and would not be for a flight uplink.

## ADR-012: New fault kinds (`reboot`, `late`, `intermittent`) and a vote deadline in the replay
**Decision:** `reboot` (silent for `down` frames, then back with its sequence counter restarted at 0) and `late` (frames leave `us` microseconds late) are new fault kinds; every fault accepts `period`/`duty` to be intermittent. `tfc_replay` groups frames the way FC-A drains them: everything received between one 7 ms vote and the next (`--vote-us`, default 7000), not by 10 ms wall-clock buckets.
**Why:** reintegration needs a node that really comes back (`reboot`, with the restarted counter a real reboot produces); the timing family needs a fault that makes data stale rather than absent (`late`: a command leaving at 9.3 ms misses the 7 ms vote and is judged a frame late, with a stale digest); and intermittent faults are exactly what a 3-of-5 window cannot see (characterised in the tests; ADR for the alpha-count follows). Without the vote deadline the replay would call a frame that FC-A would reject "on time".
**Effect on existing results:** scheduled frames all leave before 7 ms, so no latch frame moves. Babble frames (8.0 ms) now land in the following frame: the replay raises the bus alarm one frame later than the live firmware, which sees them as they arrive.

## ADR-013: A leaky alpha-count catches intermittent faults the 3-of-5 window cannot see
**Decision:** Every node also feeds a leaky score (`AlphaCount`): +1 per bad frame, x 0.9 per good frame, latch at 3.0, OR'd with the existing 3-of-5 window. A latch the window did not cause carries the reason `intermittent fault`. That reason is in the *physical* class (`physical_causes`), so a repeat disables the node at the second strike, and it is never readmitted by the automatic policy. Fixed-point (Q16) arithmetic. `alpha_threshold = 0` switches it off.
**Why (measured, ADR-010 audit):** a node corrupting 20-40% of its frames for a whole run was never isolated: 3-of-5 needs three bad frames inside five, and a node that is bad one frame in three or two in five never gives it that. The vote masks each bad frame so the *output* stayed right, but the node was plainly sick and one more fault on another node would have been unmaskable. Real faults of this kind are common (loose connectors, marginal parts, EMI), which is why the fault-handling literature separates transient from intermittent from permanent.
**Research:** the α-count of Bondavalli, Chiaradonna, Di Giandomenico and Grandoni (IEEE Trans. Computers, 2000; read through search summaries of the abstract, not the full paper): +1 per error, multiplied by K (0 <= K <= 1) per success, threshold αT, diagnosing an intermittent or permanent fault; two thresholds improve it (not implemented: strikes and probation already give a second stage). AUTOSAR DEM's debouncing counters are the same family.
**Parameters, chosen by simulation (K, T) against fault patterns, then confirmed in the manager:**

| Pattern (bad frames) | 3-of-5 alone | alpha K=0.9, T=3 | measured in the replay (fault at 100) |
|---|---|---|---|
| single glitch, two-frame burst | never | never | never |
| 1 in 20, 1 in 10, 1 in 5 | never | never | never (no false isolation) |
| 3-frame burst (a hard fault) | on the 3rd frame | on the 3rd frame | unchanged: the window gets there first |
| 1 in 3 (33%) | never | 12 frames after start | frame 112 |
| 2 in 5 (40%) | never | 6 frames after start | frame 106 |
| 2 in 10 (20%, paired) | never | 21 frames after start | frame 121 |

Other settings tried: K=0.9/T=4 misses 1-in-5 and 2-in-10; K=0.95/T=4 catches 1-in-5 only after 40 frames and 2-in-10 after 31; K=0.8/T=3 misses 1-in-3. K=0.9, T=3 was chosen as a judgement call from this table: a node that is bad one frame in five or less (20%) is left alone, while one in three (33%) and two in five (40%) are isolated within about a dozen frames. A more patient setting (K=0.95, T=4) would also catch one in five, after 40 frames, at the price of a slower response elsewhere; where that line belongs depends on real error rates and is a tuning question for the rig. A long-run guard test (a glitch every 23 frames for 5,000 frames) proves a healthy node is never latched.
**Why OR with the window, and Q16:** the window is exact and fast for bursts and already specified (FDIR-004); the leaky count only adds what it cannot see, so hard-fault timing is unchanged (tested). Fixed-point because every replica must reach the same verdict from the same frames and float rounding is a needless way to disagree.
**Why an intermittent latch is "physical":** it recurs by definition; readmitting it once is reasonable (strike 1) but a second latch of the same kind means failing hardware, so the node is disabled instead of cycling through probation again and again.
**Limits:** the constants are tuned on a simulation of frame-level faults; real error rates will differ and must be re-tuned from measurements on the rig (the bench checks and the fault campaign). It judges frame-level badness only (missing, CRC, sequence, vote, digest, stuck); it does not look at sensor-noise statistics. No second threshold yet.


## ADR-014: Total loss is recoverable: probationers judge each other when nobody is healthy
**Decision:** If no node is `Healthy`, the nodes on probation use the vote *among themselves* as the reference instead of the (non-existent) voted output of the healthy nodes: each frame their data are voted (median, tolerance as usual); a node that is the odd one out, on a channel or in its digest, fails the probation; two that cannot be told apart (a duplex miscompare, three different values) make the frame neutral, so it neither counts nor fails. The "one probation at a time" rule is lifted *in that state only*. A node is still put on probation only by an operator request after its dwell, and still needs 100 (300 after a repeat) agreeing frames; one such cohort needs **at least two** members. As soon as one member is readmitted it is Healthy and the others continue against the ordinary shadow vote.
**Why (found by the fault campaign, group `total_loss`):** every node latched at once (a three-frame outage that hits all of them, or the last healthy node failing after the others were already out) was **unrecoverable**: probation compares a node with the voted output of the healthy nodes, there were none, so every frame was neutral for ever and the operator's commands changed nothing. ADR-010 had listed this as a known limit ("needs a restart"); the campaign showed how small the trigger is (3 frames) and that the restart is the *only* way out, i.e. one bus glitch ends the flight's redundancy for good. On the real system FC-A's own frames do not cross the bus, so the trigger there is "FC-A's own data are bad while B and C are out", rarer but not impossible.
**Why this design:** the alternatives were a *reset* command (needs a new operation, loses strikes and history, and an operator can already do it by power-cycling), an *automatic* restart when everything is latched (a decision to trust data that nobody has checked), or readmitting a lone survivor (it has nothing to be checked against). Mutual agreement of two or more candidates is the weakest assumption that still verifies something, and it is the same assumption the Triplex/Duplex vote already makes.
**Limits:** two nodes that fail the *same way* agree with each other (common-cause; no vote can help, see ADR-006 and the correlated-fault results); one candidate alone cannot be judged and waits; while Safe is requested (sticky) every frame has `held` outputs and is neutral, so the operator must clear Safe first.
**Tests:** `total_loss_*` (six C++ tests), `test_F46_*` (replay), campaign group `total_loss`, and four mutants (`cohort_*`).

## ADR-015: The manager protects its own state and configuration
**Decision:** (1) `RedundancyConfig` is validated and *sanitised* at construction: every invalid field (a tolerance that is not a finite positive number, M-of-N outside 1 <= M <= N <= 32, a stuck limit under 2, leaky-count constants out of range, an arbitration factor between 0 and 1, probation or strike limits that contradict each other) is replaced by its default, and the manager reports which (`config_errors()`, a `CONFIG ERROR` line on the console). (2) Node states and the Safe flag are stored with their bitwise complement (`GuardedByte`); the configuration is stored twice with a checksum over its field values. (3) At the start of every frame a *scrub* checks them: a node state that fails is set to `Latched` (excluded, recoverable by command), a Safe flag that fails is set to *requested*, a configuration that fails is restored from its second copy (or the defaults) **and Safe is requested**, an impossible command-queue length is clamped. Each repair is counted (`integrity_faults`), reported in the frame (`integrity_mask`) and printed. (4) Out-of-range node indices and operations are refused, never indexed.
**Why:** a redundancy manager is the one component whose wrong belief is not outvoted by anything, and the RAM of a flight computer is exposed to single-event upsets (NASA-STD-8719.13 and NPR 7150.2 expect safety-critical state to be protected and checked, with a fail-safe response). The campaign's fault model stops at the bus; this is the matching defence for the manager itself. Configuration is data from outside the proven code: a zero or NaN tolerance does not fail loudly, it makes every comparison false so the voter silently stops seeing disagreement (a mutant that skips this validation is killed by the tests).
**Why complement-and-compare, not a CRC or triple storage:** one flipped bit is the dominant upset and is detected exactly by a complement; triple storage with voting could *correct* it but triples the state and adds a vote that itself needs protecting; the fail-safe repair (exclude, Safe) costs little because the operator can restore a node with `reintegrate`. A detected upset is not trusted either way. Two flips that cancel are not detected; a periodic scrub of every frame is what keeps that window short.
**Fail-safe direction chosen per item:** node state -> `Latched` (excluded), not `Healthy` (trusted) and not `Disabled` (a permanent loss for one flipped bit); Safe flag -> requested; configuration -> restored, and Safe requested because the previous frame may have been judged with a wrong limit.
**Tests:** `seu_*`, `config_*` (C++); mutants `scrub_ignores_*`, `config_not_*`, `tolerance_not_validated`, `persistence_not_validated`, `queue_length_unchecked`. **Limits:** the monitors' own internal fields (window history, leaky score, sequence trackers) are not individually guarded; an upset there causes at worst one wrong frame verdict, which the persistence logic already tolerates. Protecting the *code* (flash ECC, program-counter watchdogs) is a hardware matter.

## ADR-016: A fault campaign with safety properties, and mutation testing to prove it can fail
**Decision:** Fault coverage is demonstrated by a *campaign* (`sim/campaign`): scenario grids (every fault kind x the parameter values around each threshold x all target nodes x start frames x Triplex/Duplex/Simplex contexts x seeds, plus recovery sequences, operator commands, two-fault and cascading cases, ten-minute runs) executed through the real core, with *oracles* checked on the per-frame decisions: properties that must **always** hold (M1-M8: mode follows the healthy count, only legal state transitions, a latched node never votes, Safe is sticky, outputs finite, a held output repeats exactly, one probation at a time except in total loss), properties that hold when **one** node is faulty (S1-S4: a healthy node is never isolated, the output stays within one tolerance of the truth, no Safe request in Triplex, no readmission while the fault is active), and advisory expectations derived from each fault's parameters (isolated, ignored, "gray" near a threshold). The campaign's own correctness is established by *mutation testing*: 49 deliberate bugs are injected into a copy of the core and each must be caught, by the C++ tests (`tools/mutation/run_unit.py`) and by the campaign (`campaign.mutate`).
**Why:** a list of hand-picked fault scenarios proves that those scenarios work. The user asked for every fault with many inputs and for irregularities to be found; properties checked over thousands of generated cases find the cases nobody thought of, and the first run did: a total-loss deadlock, a frozen command that got the healthy node isolated, a three-way digest split that latched every node. Mutation testing answers "would this test notice if the code were wrong?": the first version of the campaign missed 4 of 12 mutants (a hold that returned zero, a probation that could not fail, a strike limit that never disabled, a second simultaneous probation), which is how the M7/M8 oracles and the `commands_strikes` group came to exist.
**Phase sweeps:** a fault's effect can depend on *when* it starts (the vehicle's motion is sinusoidal, so what a frozen value looks like depends on the slope at that instant). The group `phase_sweep` starts every one of the 32 fault kinds at 40 different instants in Triplex and in Duplex. The defect in ADR-017 hid from the first campaign because `cmdstuck` had been tried at one start frame only, where the slope was gentle.
**Determinism as a regression oracle:** every scenario is exactly reproducible from its seed, and the results carry a hash of every per-frame decision. A refactor is proven behaviour-preserving by running the campaign before and after: across the split of `end_frame` into small steps, the new self-protection and the configuration validation, 8,033 of 8,049 shared scenarios had identical hashes, and the 16 that differed were exactly the scenarios of the two intended behaviour changes (12 total-loss recoveries and 4 `clear-disabled` dwell cases). The later change of ADR-017 altered 415 scenarios on purpose; each was reviewed (no property violated, five cases lost an isolation to a Safe request at a threshold magnitude).
**Limits:** the oracles judge frame-level behaviour on a synthetic motion profile and a synthetic estimator digest; timing is virtual; electrical and bus-arbitration effects need the rig. An expectation near a threshold is "gray" (reported, not judged) because noise decides which side it lands on.

## ADR-017: Duplex arbitration compares the nodes with where the signal should be, not where it was
**Decision:** `arbitrate()` judges the two surviving nodes against a *predicted* reference: the last agreed value plus the last agreed step (`2 x last - previous`), when two consecutive trusted frames exist; otherwise the bare last value as before. Blame still requires one node farther than `duplex_arbitration_factor` x tolerance from the reference while the other is within one tolerance; a stale reference (after a hold) still never blames anyone.
**Why (found by the fault campaign, group `phase_sweep`):** ADR-008 compared the nodes with the last agreed value. That works while the signal moves less than a tolerance per frame (the gyro and accel channels) but not on the command channels, where the tolerance is 0.01 deg and the command moves up to 0.05 deg per frame: a node that stopped following the motion (a *frozen command*, a stale or replayed frame) then sat exactly on the last agreed value and looked like the stable one, while the **healthy** node looked like the one that "jumped" more than 2 tolerances. In 63 of 80 start phases the healthy node was isolated, the faulty node became the only voter and **its frozen command became the output**, with no Safe request: a wrong actuator command from a single fault. `late`, `replay`, `cmdinvert`, `jitter` and `clockdrift` also put one wrong output through in Duplex before being attributed. In the other direction, `cmd_offset` in Duplex was *never* attributed (all 42 cases ended in Safe) because the healthy node's own motion exceeded one tolerance from the standstill reference. With the predicted reference the phase sweep has no violation of any property in any kind, and `cmd_offset` in Duplex is isolated in all 42 cases.
**Amended after the E19 measurement (see the end of this ADR):** the prediction is now used only where the signal really moves. **Cost and limits (of the first version):** the prediction adds the noise of two agreed samples (about 2.2 sigma for a gyro); at a fault magnitude exactly on the 2 x tolerance boundary the outcome (isolate or Safe) can flip with the noise, as it could before: of the cases that changed, five lost an isolation to a Safe request at those boundary magnitudes. Where the signal barely moves (the peaks of the sinusoid, slope under two tolerances per frame) neither node can be singled out and the system still holds and requests Safe, which is the fail-safe outcome. A commanded step is seen identically by both nodes, so it never reaches the arbitration. **Alternatives rejected:** a configured slew allowance per channel (needs the vehicle's dynamics, which the manager does not know and which differ per stage); a longer filter (more lag, more noise); trusting the node with the lower variance (a frozen node has zero variance).
**E19 amendment, measured:** sweeping the bias in Duplex over 60 seeds per magnitude showed that on the gyro channels (steps under half a tolerance per frame) the prediction only added noise: the isolate-or-Safe threshold moved from 2.0x tolerance (50% isolated) to about 2.3x and the transition widened from about 0.2x to 0.3x. The reference now adds the last agreed step with a weight that is 0 below one tolerance per frame, ramps to 1 at two tolerances per frame and stays 1 above (`reference()` in `redundancy.hpp`). Gyro and accel channels therefore behave exactly as before ADR-017: the per-frame decision hashes of all 531 Duplex scenarios of the groups `bias_gyro`, `bias_accel`, `drift`, `noise`, `scale`, `saturate` and `stuck` are identical to those of the core from before ADR-017. The command channels keep the E16 fix. Tests `reference_*`, mutants `reference_always_predicts`, `reference_never_predicts`.
**Tests:** `duplex_a_frozen_command_*` (C++, three), `test_F50_*` (replay, every phase), the 2,560-scenario `phase_sweep` group, and mutants `arbitration_standstill_reference` and `arbitration_extrapolates_backwards`.


## ADR-018: Frames carry SYNC's frame number, and the receiver checks the phase
**Decision:** The 8-bit `seq` field of every sensor and command frame is the low byte of the number of the SYNC frame that opened the cycle the frame belongs to, not a counter the node keeps for itself. The receiver (`PhaseTracker`, one per node and stream) compares it with the number of the frame it is collecting and keeps a 32-cycle history of which numbers arrived and which cycles delivered anything at all: the current number is **on time** (once); an earlier number (up to 31 back) that was not seen before and whose own cycle came up **empty** is a **late** frame (it missed the vote: one lost sample, its data are stale and the vote and digest judge them as before); anything else is **bad**: a number from the future, too old, a repeat, or an earlier number although its cycle already delivered a frame (a stream that is permanently one number behind). Bad frames set the `sequence` reason and are counted in `seq_bad`. The manager keeps its own frame count (`frame_number()`, incremented by `end_frame()`), or takes it from `begin_frame(k)`; the firmware passes SYNC's number.
**Why (campaign edge case E11):** the old counter only counted frames, so a node whose frames left more than about 4.5 ms early (or a clock drifting early) delivered a perfectly contiguous stream one whole frame ahead of the others: no sequence error, no missing frame, and a data error under one tolerance on this motion. The receiver's acceptance window is a whole cycle wide for a frame that takes 0.1 ms on the wire, so *when* a frame was sent could not be seen at all. The number says which frame the data belong to; a stream that is out of phase by one or more whole frames is now isolated in 3 frames, whatever the cause (early, late, replayed, duplicated, frozen counter, wrong frame number after a missed SYNC).
**Effects on the system:** no change to the frame format, the schedule or the bus load (the same byte is filled differently); a node that reboots or joins late takes the number from SYNC and is in phase at once, so a restart is no longer a sequence break (the `reboot` fault comes back in phase; `resync=0` models a node that does not resync and restarts from 0, which is isolated); the logic of "lost frame, damaged frame, late burst" got simpler (no `note_missing`/`note_damaged` bookkeeping across frames: absolute numbers make a lost frame leave no trace on the next one). `seqgap` now means "the frame number is off by `gap` for as long as the fault is active", so a permanent offset is a node out of phase and a windowed one is a one-frame glitch that 3-of-5 tolerates.
**A side benefit, measured:** the number is judged per node, not by comparing nodes, so a frame-level timing or replay fault is attributable even in Duplex, where a value comparison cannot say who is wrong. In the campaign, `late` in Duplex went from 0 of 40 isolated (all ended in Safe) to 40 of 40 isolated, `replay` from 21 to 33 of 33, and `clockdrift` and `early` are detected in 1 to 3 frames.
**Requirements this puts on the firmware of nodes B and C (docs/design/FUTURE_WORK.md):** each node takes the frame number from SYNC (not from its own counter), keeps counting if SYNC is lost (and the sync-master takeover must keep the number continuous), and stamps the number of the cycle the data were sampled in. ACT should apply the same check.
**Limits:** it checks the phase in whole frames. A frame that is up to about 4.5 ms early (or 5.5 ms late for a gyro frame) still arrives in the right window with the right number and is accepted: seeing that needs the arrival time against the slot, which needs receive timestamps on the target (E11 option B, deferred, telemetry first). A frame at frame 0 that is numbered one behind cannot be told from a late frame of a cycle that never existed (nothing to contradict it). A 1-byte number wraps every 2.56 s, which is far longer than the 32-cycle history.
**Alternatives rejected:** keep the counter and add a per-node phase offset estimate (a learned offset is a state that can itself be wrong, and it hides a node that was never in phase); a 32-bit number (needs the 4 spare bytes of the frame, which are the data; the byte is enough for an equality check); timestamps in the payload (the sender's clock is exactly the thing being checked).
**Tests:** `phase_*` (C++, protocol), `frame_number_*`, `test_F43_*`, `test_a_node_whose_frame_number_stays_wrong_*`, the campaign groups `seqgap`, `early`, `reboot`, and mutants `phase_*`.

## ADR-019: Ground commands are authenticated, replay-protected and two-step; disabling has an interlock
**Decision (campaign edge case E10):** (1) **Tag.** Bytes 2-5 of the ground frame carry the low 32 bits of SipHash-2-4 over (frame id, opcode byte, node, counter) with a shared 128-bit key (`auth.hpp`; test vectors of the SipHash paper pass in C++ and in Python). A frame whose tag does not verify is dropped without a trace on the bus and counted in `commands_unauthentic`. (2) **Counter.** The counter byte must be 1 to `command_window` (32) ahead of the last accepted one (modulo 256); a repeat or an older one is a replay (`commands_replayed`); commands lost on the way are tolerated up to the window. (3) **ARM/EXECUTE.** Setting bit 7 of the opcode makes the frame an ARM for that operation and node; the matching EXECUTE must follow within `arm_window_frames` (250 = 2.5 s), and one ARM covers one EXECUTE. `clear-disabled` and `clear-safe` always need it, because both undo a protective action. (4) **Interlock tiers for `disable`:** of a node that is voting, with three healthy nodes (Triplex to Duplex) it is a plain command; with two (Duplex to Simplex) it needs an ARM; with one (the last voter) it needs an ARM and is reported loudly (`CRITICAL` on the console and in the report, counter `critical_commands`). Disabling a node that is not voting (latched, on probation) is plain. `reintegrate` never needs an ARM: the shadow vote is its check.
**Why:** the frame had a CRC and nothing else, so (a) a stale frame replayed by a stuck transmitter, a gateway or a recorded log could re-apply an old `disable` or lift a legitimate Safe request; (b) a corrupted frame that still passes CRC-8, or a node stuck on that id sending random payloads, passes a valid opcode and node with probability of about 7 in 10 million per frame (about one wrongly accepted command every few minutes at bus speed); (c) nothing stopped an operator, or a script, from disabling every node, one command at a time; (d) any node on the bus could issue a command. The tag closes (b) and (d) on the bench, the counter closes (a), the ARM closes the single-corrupted-frame class and gives (c) a deliberate second step, and the tiers keep (c) possible: in Duplex the manager cannot attribute a small fault (E6) and the way out is the operator isolating the node they know is bad, which a hard "never below two healthy nodes" refusal would have forbidden. This follows what flight programs do (hazardous commands are armed and then executed; the link is authenticated with sequence numbers; the internal bus is treated as a trusted segment).
**Rejected:** a hard refusal below two healthy nodes (blocks the E6 recovery); a MAC with a key per node (key management for a bench); AES-GCM (a hardware block this MCU may not have, no benefit for 5 bytes); counters kept in flash (the first accepted command after a reboot re-establishes the window, an acceptable gap).
**Key handling (bench):** the default key is the public SipHash test key `00..0f`, so the tools, the tests and the firmware agree out of the box. It protects nothing. The firmware takes `CONFIG_TFC_GROUND_KEY`, the host tools `$TFC_GROUND_KEY`; a configuration with authentication on and an all-zero key is replaced by the bench key and reported. Provisioning, storage and rotation of a real key are not designed (docs/design/FUTURE_WORK.md).
**Self-protection:** the counter record and the ARM (code and timer) are guarded bytes, scrubbed every frame like the node states (ADR-015); a damaged counter record forgets the history (the next authentic command re-establishes it; fail-open on replay protection only, never on authenticity), a damaged ARM is cleared.
**Limits:** a 32-bit tag allows 2^32 guesses to forge; the comparison is not constant-time (irrelevant on a bench, relevant for a real uplink); a replay inside the window of a command *newer* than the last accepted one cannot exist by construction, but a recorded command whose counter is within the window and ahead of the last accepted one would be accepted (the usual limit of a counter window; a real uplink also needs freshness); there is no confidentiality. Authentication of the real uplink belongs at the vehicle's gateway (CCSDS-style link security), and all of this assumes the three flight computers see the same frames (inconsistent omission would leave their counters different; the window tolerates it, membership agreement would fix it).
**Tests:** `ground_*`, `arm_*`, `interlock_*` (C++), `siphash24_*`, `test_F52_*`, `test_F53_*`, the campaign group `ground_security` and `commands_*`, the fuzz operator (forged tags, stale counters, ARMed and un-ARMed commands), `seu_*` for the command state, and mutants `ground_*`, `arm_*`, `interlock_*`.

## ADR-020: Sensing: sensor health is separated from computer health (accepted); a ring re-homing of IMUs is a later stretch; no simultaneous multi-master IMU wiring
**Context:** each flight computer reads one IMU (SPI) and every one receives all three through the sensor exchange, so the three IMUs are already *logically* cross-strapped: any computer can use any IMU's sample. The owner's two requirements: **(1)** if an IMU breaks, its flight computer should not become useless; **(2)** if IMU A and flight computer B both break, flight computer A should be able to read IMU B. Today neither holds. For (1), the fault manager's unit of isolation is the node, so a bad gyro latches the whole computer (checked: `B:bias` on a gyro latches node B), although its command, computed from the consensus of all three samples, may be fine. For (2), IMU B's samples reach the bus only through computer B, so they vanish with it, and sensing falls to one IMU where physical access would leave two. The lecture's cross-strapped architecture (either box can reach either set of devices) costs more wiring, connectors and ways to fail.
**Decision, case 1 (accepted):** separate **sensor-channel health from compute-channel health** in the manager (TFC-ARCH-001). A gyro or accelerometer stream is a *sensor channel* (one per IMU); a command stream is a *compute channel* (one per computer). A bad sensor channel is excluded from the consensus, and its computer stays in the command vote for as long as its commands agree; only a disagreement of its *commands* removes it. Every computer sees the same samples, so all exclude the same channel in the same frame and the replicas stay identical (the digest still agrees). The number of healthy sensor channels and of healthy compute channels are reported separately and each has its own mode. No wiring changes, no parts. The sensor and command IDs keep their values (IMU k is hosted by computer k, so `0x100+k` is already "channel k"); what changes is their meaning in the manager.
**Decision, case 2 (deferred stretch, after S4): ring re-homing.** The backup host of IMU k is computer k-1 (IMU B to A, IMU C to B, IMU A to C), which covers the owner's example and its mirrors. Each computer gets a second SPI port for the IMU it backs up. One quad 2:1 bus switch per IMU (the 74CBTLV3257 class; availability, voltage and on-resistance to be checked) carries its four SPI signals (SCK, MOSI, MISO, CS) and connects it to **exactly one host at a time**, so SPI stays single-master and the consensus input stays identical for all computers. The select line defaults to home (pulled), so a dead supervisor changes nothing (SUP-007). Handover: the supervisor holds the dead computer in reset, then asserts one `ADOPT` line that both flips the switch and tells the backup host to read its second SPI port and publish the orphaned channel; the adopted channel starts on **probation** (a shadow vote against the consensus, as for a node in ADR-010) before it counts. It takes seconds (the supervisor has to declare the computer dead first), so it restores sensor redundancy after a double fault; it is not seamless fail-operational (TFC-ARCH-003).
**Decided now, because it is cheap now and expensive later (rule 10-11 of STAGED_BUILD):** (a) sensor frames identify the **channel**, not the sender, and at any time one publisher per channel (TFC-ARCH-009); (b) each IMU is powered from a rail that does not depend on its home computer being powered, otherwise it dies with it (TFC-ARCH-010: a small regulator per IMU from the node rail upstream of the relays, or a diode-OR of the home and backup hosts' 3.3 V; check the Adafruit board's logic level against the Nucleo's 3.3 V when choosing); (c) per-channel calibration constants are known to every computer (TFC-ARCH-011); (d) each node's harness reserves the second SPI pins (SPI1 on the Nucleo shares its clock pin with the LED, compatibility audit item 5: use other instances) and an `ADOPT` input.
**Why not simultaneous direct wiring (every computer reads every IMU at once):** (a) SPI has one master per slave; three masters need a switch or a multi-master I2C bus, where one stuck master hangs every IMU (a common cause across all three). (b) The sensor has, as far as the search of its documentation showed, no external sample trigger; computers reading the same IMU at different instants would get different samples, so the consensus inputs would differ, which breaks the bit-identical-replica assumption and the digest check (ARCHITECTURE section 4) unless the samples are exchanged over the bus anyway. (c) On a shared I2C bus nine reads at 400 kHz would not fit the 1.0 ms between the sample latch and the first sensor slot; on SPI the time is there but the wiring is not. (d) The lecture's own caution: more options, but more wiring, connectors and ways to fail. The ring above keeps one owner at a time instead.
**Alternatives:** *smart IMU nodes* (each IMU on its own small CAN node: sensing and computing become separate boxes and every computer receives the same frames by construction, so case 2 needs no switch; three more microcontrollers and CAN transceivers, about 72 USD at the parts-sheet prices for Nucleos, less with cheaper boards that are not priced here; the closest to real avionics, over budget); *a fourth, cold-spare IMU* (does not separate the failure modes); *the ring with a full 3x3 crosspoint* (any computer can adopt any IMU; many more switches for little gain).
**Consequences:** the manager's per-node state gets a second dimension, a real change to `core/`, the campaign oracles and the mutants. Case 1 can be written and tested in simulation now, because the virtual peers' gyro faults already leave the command stream untouched (F65); it becomes meaningful end to end with the estimator. Risks of case 2: the switch is a new element that can fail (default-home and the supervisor's logic bound it); an IMU that shorts its own SPI lines affects only its own bus until adopted; the supervisor's seconds-long handover. **Requirements:** TFC-ARCH-001 to 003, 009 to 011. **Tests:** F65, F73, F74.
**Built (5 Oct 2026):** case 1 is in the manager as `RedundancyConfig::sensor_split` (`core/include/tfc/sensor_health.hpp`: each IMU channel has the life cycle of a computer, ADR-010, 013 and 014, judged on the six sensor votes; the computers are judged on the command frames, the command votes and the digest; operator commands address an IMU by node 4 to 6, with its own ARM tier), on in the firmware with the flight function, and measured by TS-15 (option B: all three computers keep voting after one IMU fault, no new Safe request in any class). Case 2 (the ring) stays a deferred stretch after S4.
**Order (owner, 4 Oct 2026):** the sensor and compute health split is done after the loop (P1) and before stage S3 (3 Nov), as its own change; the estimator is designed now to take channels with a valid flag so the split slots in. The degradation rule (what happens to a computer's command once its sensing is down to two channels) is decided by trade study TS-15 before the split is written.

## ADR-021 (accepted 4 Oct 2026: hold and ask; all of it accepted 5 Oct 2026): The third computer runs the previous known-good release; a disagreement between releases holds the output and asks for an operator
**Context:** three identical computers running identical software survive a hardware fault and not a software bug: a bug in the release makes all three wrong the same way, and the vote agrees on the wrong answer. Measured on the current voter: with B and C given the same `cmd_offset`, the healthy A is latched out at frame 102 and the system continues in Duplex on the two wrong nodes (F63). The Space Shuttle's answer was a fifth computer running separately developed software, used only if the whole primary set failed, monitoring the primary all along. Independent versions are not independent in practice (the Knight and Leveson experiment found independently written versions fail together more often than independence predicts), and an *older release of the same code* is weaker diversity still: it shares the specification, the algorithms and most of the code. What it does protect against is a **regression introduced since the known-good release**, which is the common case.
**Decision (accepted):** Nodes A and B run the current release; node C runs the **golden release**, a tagged release that passed the full campaign and the hardware stage tests, built reproducibly, changed only for safety fixes (TFC-ARCH-005). **A plain 2-of-3 vote cannot deliver the protection**: if the regression is common to A and B they agree, outvote the correct C, and the vote *isolates the healthy node*. So (TFC-ARCH-007): when two nodes of one release agree and the node of the other release disagrees beyond a **version tolerance** for M of N frames, **nobody is isolated, the output holds, and a Safe request is raised**; the operator resolves it by naming the node to trust (`disable` the other pair under an ARM, then `clear-safe`), exactly as for an unattributable Duplex disagreement (ADR-008). It does not pick a side because nothing in the data can say who is right.
**Needed for this to work:** (1) a frozen bus interface: a protocol version and the release's source hash in each heartbeat, and compatibility of the golden release with the current protocol (TFC-ARCH-004); (2) a digest over a version-stable, quantised state so the cross-check does not flag every release difference (TFC-ARCH-008; today the digest is over the whole estimator state and would mismatch by design); (3) a **behavioural-compatibility gate** in CI: replay the scenario set through both releases and require the commands to agree within the version tolerance, so a release that legitimately changes behaviour is noticed before it is fielded against the old golden (TFC-ARCH-006); (4) the mixed configuration in the test plan: three `native_sim` builds with different node numbers and releases on one `vcan0`, no hardware needed.
**Alternatives:** *vote only* (no protection against the case it is for); *golden as a monitor that takes over automatically when a plausibility envelope is exceeded by the pair* (the Shuttle model; more protection, but the envelope is a second model of the vehicle and a new thing that can be wrong; keep as a later option); *independent re-implementation of the estimator by a second author* (genuine diversity, a large effort, and a large project risk).
**Limits to state in the write-up:** protects against regressions, not against a bug present in both releases, a specification error, or an input-dependent bug (a warm spare shares those too: `MISSION_PHASES.md`). Diversity costs coverage: the golden release is a frozen build, so its own bugs are not fixed.
**Consequences:** the campaign's single-fault oracle S1 ("a healthy node is never isolated") is correct for single faults; the common-mode case is a *two-node* fault and needs its own scenario (F63). **Requirements:** TFC-ARCH-004 to 008. **Tests:** F63, F64; the campaign group `common_mode`.
**Built (5 Oct 2026):** `RedundancyConfig::release_aware` and `version_tol_factor`; the manager reads each computer's release from its heartbeat, treats a lone computer of another release against the pair's mid-value within the version tolerance, does not compare its digest with the pair's, and beyond the tolerance isolates nobody, holds the outputs and requests Safe (`reason`, `release_split_frames`); `tools/compat` is the compatibility gate (TFC-ARCH-006) and `tools/release/record_release.py` the release record (ARCH-005); TFC-ARCH-008 is realised as "digests are compared only within a release" instead of a version-stable digest. Tests: `test_release.cpp`, the campaign group `common_mode` (96 runs), `test_live_release.py`; procedure `P-REL-01`.
**Status, 4 Oct 2026:** the owner chose **hold, request Safe, operator picks** (option b). Whether the old release should instead take over automatically (option c, the Shuttle model) is decided after the TS-3 data. Until then no automatic choice between releases exists in the manager: a disagreement beyond the version tolerance is frozen and the operator names the side to trust.

## ADR-022 (accepted 4 Oct 2026: the Lite build): A supervisor ("lizard brain") with its own clock and the watchdog the flight computers cannot give themselves
**Context:** the watchdogs listed in ARCHITECTURE section 5 live inside the software they protect, and the Mars rover's Sol 200 anomaly is the case where that is not enough: enough tasks stayed alive to keep servicing the watchdog while others were hung, and recovery was a hardware command from outside the flight software. The lecture's lizard brain is separate hardware that watches the computer, can swap or reset it, accepts hardware commands that bypass the flight software, and keeps its own copy of time.
**Decision (accepted):** add a supervisor node (`docs/design/SUPERVISOR.md`): its own processor family (RP2350), its own TCXO-based clock, powered upstream of every relay. It watches each node through two discrete lines (frame start and an end-of-frame kick sent only from the end of a *completed* frame), resets or power-cycles a node whose kicks stop, holds a looping node in reset, measures each node's frame clock against its own clock, sequences boot, and takes hardware commands over USB. It acts **only through discrete lines and USB, never through the flight bus**, never votes, and is **fail-passive**. The variant with a listen-only CAN tap (SUP-Full) is an upgrade that adds arrival-time margins and frame-number plausibility.
**Why it is not the time master:** a central time source would remove the sync-master takeover but make the supervisor a single point of failure for time. SYNC stays with the flight computers; the supervisor measures it.
**Alternatives:** *rely on the on-chip watchdogs* (the failure the deck's case study shows); *the supervisor as a bus guardian that gates each node's transmitter* (needs the transceiver's silent pin, which the CAN Pal breakout may not bring out; "hold in reset" does the same job for the cases we care about); *put the supervisor on the fault injector's Pico* (the injector exists to kill nodes and must not be the thing that protects them).
**Consequences:** one more part (Pico 2 and a clock module, about 6 USD plus the module; the relay channels already bought), nine lines to the nodes, a new firmware to write and test, and a new failure mode (a spurious reset), which is the safe direction. It subsumes the isolation line of TFC-FDIR-039 and the bus-alarm response of TFC-FDIR-040. **Requirements:** TFC-SUP-001 to 010, TFC-FDIR-038. **Tests:** F57, F58, F59, F66, F67, F68.
**Built (5 Oct 2026):** the decision logic is `supervisor/include/sup/` (host-tested, 100 % line coverage, mutation-tested) and the Pico 2 application `firmware/supervisor` builds (the ladder, boot sequencing, T-zero, the clock record, the override sense lines). Run on a board: no.
**Status, 4 Oct 2026:** the owner chose **SUP-Lite** (a second Pico 2 and a TCXO clock module, discrete lines only); SUP-Full stays an upgrade path. TS-0 recorded this. Consequence: TFC-SUP-005 (arrival margins from outside the computers) and the frame-number half of TFC-SUP-009 are Full-only and are not built; the arrival-time telemetry stays inside the computers (FDIR-037).

## ADR-023 (accepted 5 Oct 2026; the second ACT is a stretch outside the v1 scope): The claim is fail-operational through sensing and computing, fail-passive beyond; mission phases set how many computers are hot; Safe is hold, then null
**Decision (accepted):** (1) **Scope of fail-operational:** after any one fault in a flight computer, an IMU, or a computer's link to the bus, the voted output continues within TFC-SYS-004 with no interruption of more than one frame (TFC-SYS-006). It is **not** claimed for the shared CAN bus, the actuator node, the servo power rail or the supervisor: those are fail-passive (hold, then null) and are listed as known limitations. A second ACT with a hardware output selector driven by the supervisor would extend the claim to the actuator node; it costs one Nucleo, one CAN Pal and a selector chip (about 24 USD plus the chip) and is a stretch decision (see `FUTURE_WORK.md`). The servo, its rail and the platform are the plant, not the flight computer, and are outside the claim. (2) **Phases:** each node has a role (HOT, WARM, COLD) separate from its health, and a phase table fixes how many are hot in each phase (`docs/design/MISSION_PHASES.md`). Triplex is required in the phases that cannot be repeated; WARM is the existing shadow-vote probation entered by phase; promotion uses the probation criteria. (3) **Safe** is fail-passive: freeze at once, then null at a limited rate after `T_hold`, with ACT able to do it alone (`docs/design/SAFE_MODE.md`).
**Why:** it states plainly what the project does and does not tolerate, using the lecture's terms (fail-safe pulls over; fail-operational keeps driving because the spare was already turning), and each statement is testable.
**Alternatives:** claim full fail-operational (false while ACT is single); no phases (loses the warm-spare and promotion demonstration and the go/no-go gate); hold-forever Safe (no defined end).
**Requirements:** TFC-SYS-006, TFC-SAFE-001 to 007, TFC-PHASE-001 to 004.
**Status, 4 Oct 2026:** the open questions of `SAFE_MODE.md` and `FAULT_RESPONSE.md` were answered by the owner (null in ascent and depower on the pad; Safe does not end the simulated run; the 0.5 s hold is a parameter that TS-4 tunes; Simplex in ascent alerts only; ACT resumes from its stored output after a reset, to be confirmed by the servo no-signal measurement; the phase minimums stand; the supervisor never resets the last agreeing computer on its own). Phases (`MISSION_PHASES.md`) and the second ACT remained proposals then.
**Built (5 Oct 2026):** the phase table, the `phase`, `warm` and `noop` ground operations, the WARM role (the probation of ADR-010, held until promoted), the interlock tiers that follow the phase, and the below-minimum alert are in the manager (`CONFIG_TFC_PHASES` in the firmware; tests `test_phases.cpp`, 32 mutants, a live test on `vcan0`). "Simplex in ascent alerts only": below a phase's minimum the manager reports and does not act. **Safe per phase:** ACT tells the pad from flight by the mission frame in SYNC: Safe on the pad goes to neutral at once, in flight it freezes, holds and ramps (`SAFE_MODE.md` section 5). **Not built:** the coast phase's "freeze only" (ACT does not know the manager's phase, which the heartbeat does not carry) and the Pico depowering the platform; COLD is the supervisor's `hold`, by hand; a second ACT stays a stretch outside v1.

## ADR-024 (accepted 4 Oct 2026): The servos are the platform; the simulator is a C++ library with a runner; the vehicle is a full 6-DOF ascent
**Context:** the documents disagreed about what the two servos do. The overview said the actuator node drives a servo; the architecture said they tilt the platform that carries the IMUs, driven by the Pico from the simulator. The parts sheet has exactly two servos, described as the platform's pitch and yaw axes, so only the second can be true.
**Decision (owner, 4 Oct 2026):** (1) **The servos are the platform**, driven by the Pico from the simulator's attitude. ACT has no PWM output: it votes the three commands and puts the voted gimbal command on the bus, and its Safe action is the value of that command. The platform has its own protection in the Pico (rate and travel limits, a watchdog on the PC link) and the E-stop. (2) **The simulator is a C++ library plus a small runner** that speaks SocketCAN and USB; Python is for scenario files and analysis. One vehicle model serves the tests, the campaign, the trade-study sweeps and the rig, so there is nothing to keep in step. (3) **The physics is a full 6-DOF ascent** (position, velocity, attitude, variable mass and inertia, thrust with altitude, TVC, atmosphere, wind, aerodynamic instability, engine-out), which the owner chose over the two-plane attitude model that had been recommended as sufficient. The platform can reproduce only two of the rotations (the long axis's tilt in the pitch and yaw planes, within about 45 degrees); roll is not reproduced, and the accelerometer on the platform reads gravity, not the vehicle's specific force, so the simulator also has a **vehicle-true sensor mode** for software-only runs.
**Why the 6-DOF choice is still worth stating plainly:** it costs about a week and the rig cannot show most of what it adds; its value is credibility (a real max-Q, a real gravity turn, a mass that changes by a factor of five) and a model whose plant changes through the flight, which forces gain scheduling, the thing a real controller needs. The risk is the schedule: if it slips, the fallback is the two-plane model already in `tools/vehicle`, behind the same interface.
**Consequences:** the Safe-mode and fault-response documents are corrected (this change); the vehicle simulator has its own design (`docs/design/VEHICLE_SIM.md`); the controller gets a gain schedule and the guidance table grows to 16 points; the protocol gains the simulator's frames (`0x501` to `0x504`) and ACT's output frame (`0x300`); the Pico firmware becomes a platform driver first and a fault injector second. **Requirements:** TFC-SIM-001 to 006, TFC-PLAT-001 to 004.

## ADR-025: SYNC following, takeover by staggered windows, and silence until a frame number exists
**Decision:** Nodes B and C follow A's SYNC: the frame is locked to the arrival of SYNC and its number is SYNC's (`tfc::SyncClock`, `core/include/tfc/sync_clock.hpp`). A follower that misses SYNC keeps counting, so the number keeps pace with time; after **two** missed frames in a row, and only if it has heard a SYNC at some time, it becomes the master and sends SYNC itself. Who goes first when several would is settled by time: the wait for SYNC lasts `1.0 + stagger x node` ms (stagger 0.5 ms on the board, `CONFIG_TFC_SYNC_STAGGER_US`; 2.5 ms on the host, whose receive latency is up to 1 ms), so the lowest node's SYNC reaches the others before their own windows end and they follow it. A master that hears another's SYNC yields. **A node that is not synced (it has not heard a SYNC and is not the master) stays silent**: it has no frame number to stamp its frames with. Node A always starts by listening (5 frames) and claims the bus only if nobody is sending, so an A that restarts while B is the master follows B instead of colliding with it (two transmitters of one id are a bus error). B and C never claim an empty bus.
**Why:** ADR-018 made the frame number SYNC's, so every node needs it, and SYNC was a single point of failure (F15). The first version let unsynced nodes free-run and transmit; on a power-up all three nodes then numbered their frames by their own counters, which disagreed by one at the moment A claimed the bus, and A latched B and C for sequence errors within the first frames (found by running three instances). Silence until synced removes the case. Time-staggered windows avoid an election protocol: nothing is exchanged, and the order of the nodes is the order of the windows.
**Deviations from STAGED_BUILD rule 3 ("sync master = lowest healthy FC"):** a returning A does not take the role back; it follows whoever is master. Taking it back would need a handover that does not exist; the master changes only by SYNC being lost.
**Limits:** SYNC carries no sender id, so a master cannot tell *who* else is sending; it yields to any SYNC it hears (a short split-brain is possible if two nodes take over within one window; the stagger is longer than the frame's time on the bus plus the receive latency, so it should not happen on a healthy bus; the first host run used 0.5 ms and the shared CI runner showed two takeovers, which is why the host stagger is 2.5 ms, and the timing of a takeover is covered in the host tests, not on the bus under load). The takeover frame is late by the window (1.5 ms for B): everyone locks to the new SYNC, so the schedule shifts once.
**A frame without SYNC is skipped, by every follower alike.** The first version ran it on the node's own clock. Because the windows differ, the followers' frames then started at different times, and with three flight computers running the real estimator two of them received different mixes of the same frames in the takeover frame (one missed the other's accelerometer sample), computed different inputs, and their estimator states drifted apart for good: the digests disagreed, the two voting nodes could not blame each other, and the system went to Safe (traced on B and C: `consensus` showed gyro nodes {B,C} and accel nodes {B} on one, {B,C} on the other). Skipping makes the sets identical (nobody sends, nobody votes); the number still counts. The flight function also uses only sensor frames that carry the current cycle's number (a late frame of an earlier cycle is stale).
**Open hole this exposed (fixed later by ADR-030):** a replicated, stateful estimator diverges for good whenever two nodes receive different sets of sensor frames in one frame, whatever the cause (a lost frame at one node, a frame that is late at one node), and the fault manager then sees a digest mismatch it cannot attribute. Healing it needs the nodes to agree on their inputs or to resynchronise their state (vote the estimator state through the state-share frames and adopt it): a design decision for a trade study before the closed loop is relied on with a lossy bus (docs/decisions/TRADE_STUDIES.md, TS-16).
**Tests:** `sync_*` (host, a model of three clocks on a perfect bus: continuity, one lost frame, B then C taking over, a restarted A following, never two masters, a never-synced follower silent), and `sim/tests/test_live_triplex.py` (three real instances; A is killed; B takes over; the numbers on the bus never repeat and skip only the one frame B counted).
**Found on the way:** the host CAN driver (`can_native_linux.c`) polls its socket every millisecond, so on native_sim a peer's frame reaches a node up to 1 ms late and a follower locks up to 1 ms late; with the vote at 7 ms about one command in eleven missed it. The host board file moves the vote to 8 ms (`CONFIG_TFC_VOTE_US`); on the board the receive is interrupt-driven and the vote stays at 7 ms.

## ADR-026 (accepted 4 Oct 2026): The fault injector's four relay channels cut the power of A, B, C and ACT; the Pico firmware is Zephyr
**Decision:** (1) The injector's four relays (option 1 of the owner's choice) are **power cuts, one each for flight computers A, B, C and the actuator node**. They use the normally-closed contacts of the ELEGOO modules, so a de-energised relay means a powered node; energising a coil is the deliberate cut. Sensor-line and bus-stub faults get **their own relay channels** (a third module: **ordered by the owner's decision of 4 Oct 2026**, `HARDWARE_PARTS.md`) and are not taken from the supervisor's four. (2) The Pico platform driver and injector is a **Zephyr application for `rpi_pico2/rp2350a/m33`** in this repository, with its limits, timeouts and relay logic written as portable code with host tests; Zephyr is kept unless the run-time check on the board shows its USB, PWM or watchdog support to be rough, in which case only the thin hardware glue is rewritten for pico-sdk.
**Why:** power cuts are what relays do well and they cover F01, F15 and F17 on real hardware; the sensor faults are already injected in software (the simulated IMU path and the campaign). One toolchain, one set of coding rules and one CI path outweigh pico-sdk's maturity for a part that is mostly portable logic.
**Check made (4 Oct 2026):** the Raspberry Pi hardware module added to `firmware/west.yml`; `rpi_pico2/rp2350a/m33` builds the Zephyr blinky, the USB CDC-ACM sample and the PWM fade sample into UF2 files. Not run on hardware: the run-time behaviour waits for the board.

## ADR-027 (accepted 5 Oct 2026): A layer of manual hardware overrides below the supervisor; TS-17 decides how many
**Decision (accepted):** add a third layer of authority that contains no code: switches and switch-driven relays that work when the flight software, the supervisor and the Pico are all down or wrong, with the priority manual over supervisor over software (`docs/design/HARDWARE_OVERRIDE.md`). The first candidates are a force-safe toggle for ACT, a platform-level switch that selects a fixed neutral servo signal, a disarm switch in the coil supply of each relay bank (so that no program can cut a node), and per-node kill switches; the E-stop of the parts list and the Nucleos' reset buttons already exist. TS-17 decides the count from a failure analysis of the switches themselves (twelve failure modes G1 to G12) and a scenario matrix.
**Decided (5 Oct 2026, TS-17 paper part, `python3 -m campaign.ts17`):** build **H2 FORCE-SAFE, H3 PLATFORM-LEVEL, H4 INJECTOR-DISARM and H5 SUPERVISOR-DISARM** beside the E-stop that the parts list already has (option O3, about 12 USD of switches, diodes and a servo-tester board; none priced on a listing). It is the smallest set in which every software-down scenario but a failing adapter has an override that needs no program. The per-node kill switches and the master switch (O4) are not built: they add no scenario that the Nucleos' reset buttons, the unpluggable CAN stubs and H4 do not already cover. **The parts are not in the order placed for 9 Oct.** The supervisor's override sense lines, the launch guard (a launch is refused while an override is engaged until `override-ok`) and the list of untested overrides are built.
**Why:** the supervisor (ADR-022) is software and is itself a failure mode; a bug common to everything the project wrote has no layer below it; the lecture's hardware command that bypasses the flight software is here taken to its end, a command that bypasses *all* software. The cost is small (about 25 USD) and the real cost is the switches' own failures (a latent loss of protection, an accidental activation, a platform that snaps), which is why the study counts them before building them.
**Alternatives:** *the supervisor alone* (it can fail like any program); *many overrides* (each a part that can fail to act or act by accident); *a mechanical prop* (not a switch).
**Revisit:** with the measured part of TS-17 on the rig.

## ADR-028 (accepted 5 Oct 2026): The launch sequence: a pad phase, per-IMU calibration, the supervisor as the authority for T-zero and the flight computers as the clock
**Decision (accepted, with the mission-clock part as ADR-029 settled it):** (1) A **pad phase** before flight: the vehicle clamped, each flight computer calibrating its own IMU's gyro bias over 10 s of rest and subtracting it before it sends, ACT Nominal before release. (2) A **10 s countdown** after an authenticated `launch` (ARM then EXECUTE); **scrub only**, before T-zero. (3) **Go/no-go**: every computer's `ready` bit, three healthy computers, ACT Nominal. (4) **T-zero** is signalled by the supervisor's discrete `T0` line (the PC's `launch` command until the supervisor exists); **the sync master latches it and distributes mission time in SYNC**, so every computer agrees on T by construction and a restarted computer rejoins on the right schedule; the supervisor keeps its own mission clock and checks the system's, and nothing flies on it after T-zero. (5) The sync master acts on the command, followers follow SYNC.
**Why:** the audit measured a 5 to 22 degree lift-off transient from a thrust misalignment and a vehicle that cannot fly a gyro bias above 0.19 dps; with a pad they are 1 to 4 degrees and at least 8 dps. Calibration must be per IMU and before the consensus, because IMUs differ by more than the consensus tolerance before any is faulty (LAUNCH_SEQUENCE.md section 2).
**Against the supervisor as the only runtime time source (the owner's first idea):** ADR-022 makes it fail-passive (TFC-SUP-007) and no bus transmitter, and left "the supervisor as time master" out because it would make it a single point of failure for time; a supervisor reset 60 s into the ascent would remove every computer's schedule. The hybrid keeps its independence (it decides T-zero and checks the time) without making the flight depend on it.
**Alternatives:** the supervisor as the continuous time source; a 32-bit T-zero in the command (no room in the frame); an ACT broadcast (no spare bytes in `0x300`); each computer counting from its own sight of the command (a frame of difference between computers, TS-16); a hold in the countdown (more states to test).
**Built (5 Oct 2026):** the pad calibration (`ImuCalibrator`), the mission frame in SYNC carried by `SyncClock` (follow before T-zero, count and verify in flight, a late joiner adopts it), `launch` (needs an ARM) and `scrub`, the go/no-go (`launch_gate.hpp`), the countdown in the firmware and the simulator's pad hold, and **the `T0` line**: the supervisor asserts it at its T-zero and the sync master takes a debounced rising edge in the last second of the countdown as T-zero (an early edge is refused, a line stuck high is never an edge, and the countdown running out is the fallback). Live tests on `vcan0` cover the launch, the scrub, a dying master and the T0 edge.
**Revisit:** when the supervisor runs on its board, and after TS-21.

## ADR-029 (accepted 5 Oct 2026): The supervisor keeps the clock of record (mission elapsed time) on an independent, battery-backed oscillator; schedule time stays in the flight computers
**Decision (accepted):** two clocks. **Schedule time** (frames since T-zero, in SYNC, ADR-028) stays with the replicated computers. The **clock of record**, mission elapsed time since T-zero across resets, power cuts and years, is kept by the supervisor on its own oscillator with a battery-backed RTC, correlated against the PC's UTC (and a GPS pulse per second if one is added), and distributed to the nodes by a pulse per second and a one-way serial line, never by the bus. Nothing controls on it (TFC-SUP-014).
**Why:** the owner's point that a long mission needs one independent truth of the time, which on a spacecraft is an atomic or ultra-stable clock with ground correlation. The drift table shows a TCXO is five minutes wrong in five years and the Nucleo's crystal fifty-three: the design must therefore carry **correlation**, not trust the oscillator, and must leave room for a better oscillator.
**Alternatives:** the flight computers' crystals as the clock (fifty minutes in five years, and every computer different); the supervisor as the schedule source too (a supervisor reset would remove every schedule, ADR-022); transmitting time on the bus (the supervisor must not transmit); a GPS-disciplined clock as the only source (no GPS in deep space, and a desk may not see the sky).
**Built (5 Oct 2026):** `MissionClock` and `TickExtender` (64-bit ticks from a 32-bit timer), the battery-backed T-zero record with its CRC, `MissionWatch` (the plausibility check of a node's frame count against the clock), the DS3231 conversion, and the PC-side `Correlator` (`sim/tfc_peers/timecorr.py`). **Amended 5 Oct 2026:** the distribution of time to the nodes (a pulse per second and a one-way serial line, TFC-SUP-013) is **not built and is outside v1**: no node's function depends on it (TFC-SUP-014), the Lite supervisor's pins are all used, and the PC reads the clock over USB and correlates it. A flight computer that wants MET in its telemetry waits for that line.
**Revisit:** when the first correlation data exist (the oscillator is TS-22).

## ADR-030 (accepted 5 Oct 2026): Replicated state is kept together by periodic resynchronisation to the mid-value, with a large-correction report; the digest check becomes persistence-based (TS-16 option C)
**Context:** TS-16 measured that a few lost or late sensor frames leave the replicas' states different for the rest of the flight, and that at 1 % frame loss ACT loses its vote and the flight is lost. The owner asked for option C with persistence-based detection (5 Oct 2026).
**Decision (accepted):** every `period` frames (100) the computers exchange their quantised state (twelve 16-bit words in four frames, `0x420 + 4n + k`) and each adopts the per-word mid-value of the healthy computers' states, **only if it has all of them**; a computer's distance from the vote is reported, and a large one is a bad frame for that computer in the manager (`reason::kResync`), through the existing 3-of-5 and leaky-count detectors; the digest mismatch counts only after `digest_persist_frames` frames in a row (default 1, so nothing changes until the firmware sets it). The vote averages two agreeing shares when only two computers are healthy.
**Why:** the measured result (`docs/design/RESYNC.md` section 5): a flight lost at 1 % loss flies to 10 %, with no new fault-manager concept and no round of messages inside the frame; the mid-value is the same rule as every other vote in the system; the large-correction report keeps a failing computer from being healed in silence (TS-16's criterion of weight 3).
**Alternatives:** agree on inputs before computing (option B: a round of messages in every frame, and the commands wait for it); tolerate the mismatch with a persistence window alone (option D: ruled out by the measurement, the mismatch does not decay); a contractive estimator (option E: does not cover the controller's integrator, which anti-windup holds); adopt a vote of fewer shares than the others take (rejected: it puts a computer in a state no other holds).
**Consequences:** a burst of twelve frames (about 1.6 ms) once a second on the bus and a later manager hand-over in that frame, to be checked on the rig; the state words and their scales are now part of the protocol (`PROTOCOL.md`); the diverse node cannot take part; the digest is a secondary check.
**Update, 5 Oct 2026:** built in the node loop (`CONFIG_TFC_RESYNC_PERIOD`, default 100; `CONFIG_TFC_RESYNC_GROUP` leaves the diverse computer of ADR-021 out so that the resync does not erase the differences it exists to show) and shown live on `vcan0` (a diverged computer is latched out for its commands, takes the vote at the resync and is reintegrated). The period is TS-23. One limit measured: the manager's 0.01 degree command tolerance still latches healthy computers at about 1 % loss at any period (docs/design/RESYNC.md section 6b).
**Revisit:** when the real bus loss rate is measured; if the loss rate is far below 0.01 % the period may be lengthened, and if the rig shows losses of the resync chunks themselves a request frame may be worth its cost.

## ADR-031 (accepted 6 Oct 2026): The simulator takes any vehicle: a general description read from a file, one model, the reference vehicle as an instance of it
**Context:** until 6 Oct 2026 the simulator flew one vehicle, written into the code (five identical engines, one tank, a single stage). The owner asked on 6 Oct for the software side to be finished before the hardware stage and for the simulator to work for any rocket or spacecraft: the number of engines, the masses, the thrust, the shape, the propulsion and what steers it. (The register's rule, "no more ADRs until the hardware has been measured", was the project's own; the owner's request is the reason this one is added.)
**Decision:** (1) a vehicle is **data**: stages (a structure, tanks that drain together or in turn, rules for ignition and separation, a throttle table), engines at any position (rise and tail-off, cant, cut-off, thrusters with their own direction that fire on the command), payloads that can leave, effectors (gimbal, fins, thrusters, reaction wheels), a scenario (wind, gusts, failures, a start in orbit) and the design of the flight computers' tables on the vehicle's nominal flight, in a JSON file (`vehicles/*.json`, `docs/design/VEHICLE_SPEC.md`). (2) **One model, not two:** the reference vehicle is `legacy_spec(Params{})`, an instance of the general description, so there is no second code path to keep right. (3) The rewrite is **held to the single-vehicle model it replaced**, frozen in `tests/oracle/vehicle6_legacy.hpp`: the flights agree to the last bit and the sensitivity tables reproduce to the digit; every new capability is checked against an answer the code does not produce (the rocket equation twice, `r x F`, hand sums of the centre of gravity). (4) The file reader is **strict**: every field is typed and ranged, every unknown field refused with the nearest name suggested, every error reported at once with its line and column. (5) The gain design gains two things a vehicle with little control at lift-off needs: a floor on the effectiveness it designs for (`b_min`) and an **adaptive schedule** that spends the 16 points where the gains change.
**Why:** a general description is the only way to show the flight software controls a plant it was not tuned on, and the experiments of the first day showed what it finds: a launcher that pitches past the platform's travel, a fin-steered rocket whose gains designed for zero authority ring the loop, a coast between stages that makes the gains enormous, and (found by the two-stage example, `SIM_FIDELITY.md` section 3.4) a gyro resolution on the bus that is too coarse for a slowly turning vehicle. A vehicle of one stage keeps its propellant as the total mass minus its structure, exactly as before: carrying a separate propellant number changed the rounding after 50 s and with it the quantised sensor values, moving the documented sensitivity numbers by 0.02 degree for no physical reason.
**Alternatives:** more fields on the old parameter struct (cannot say a second stage or a ring of engines); a general model beside the reference one (two models to keep right, and no oracle); a script or plug-in interface (loses a file that can be read, diffed, validated and committed); YAML or TOML (needs a library; JSON with comments and a strict 300-line reader needs none and the simulator builds without exceptions); a free-form error policy (silently ignoring an unknown field would fly a rocket the file did not describe).
**Consequences:** the simulator is 1.45 times slower on the reference vehicle (the general code; measured with `tfc_sens`); `Params` keeps its simple fields, which describe the reference vehicle, and gains `spec`; the runner and the closed loop take a design `Plan`; four tools take `--vehicle`; the aerodynamics, the planet and the environment are still the reference vehicle's (`VEHICLE_SPEC.md` section 8 lists what is built next, in order, as further changes). The flight software's own limits (two tilt planes, one gain set, 16 points, the platform's ±45 degrees) are stated, not hidden (`VEHICLE_SPEC.md` section 6).
**Revisit:** when the aerodynamics follow the shape (a change of the description's `aero`), and if the flight software's two-plane controller is ever replaced by one that steers a quaternion error.

## ADR-032 (accepted 7 Oct 2026): The gyro frames carry 1/32 degree per second per count (was 0.125), a range of +-1024 dps
**Context:** the two-stage launcher of `vehicles/` (ADR-031) showed on 6 Oct 2026 that the bus's gyro scale, 0.125 dps per count, is a bias for a vehicle that turns slowly: the launcher's pitch program turns at 0.19 dps, one and a half counts, and with the accelerometer off (a vehicle under thrust) the flight computers' attitude estimate walked 7.7 degrees away in 390 s while the loop was satisfied (`SIM_FIDELITY.md` 3.4). Same register rule as ADR-031: the owner asked on 6 Oct for the software to be finished before the hardware, and on 7 Oct chose the scale recommended there.
**Decision:** `tfc::kGyroLsbDps` is 1/32 (range +-1024 dps; the Python mirror `GYRO_LSB_DPS` is the same number), for the gyro frames `0x100+n` and the simulator's body-rate frame `0x501`. The accelerometer (1/2048 g), command (0.001 degree) and every other scale are unchanged. The heartbeat's protocol version stays 2.
**Why:** measured on the same flight (`tfc_fly`, two-stage launcher, vehicle sensors, accelerometer off): the attitude error at 390 s is 0.70 degree at 1/32, against 7.66 at 0.125 and 0.47 at 1/128. 1/32 keeps room for the platform's 300 dps rate limit and for the fault campaign's full-scale injection (1024 dps is five times what the platform can do), where 1/128 (+-256 dps) would not. It is a change of one constant on both sides of the bus, with the golden frames recomputed and pinned in C++ and Python (the Python made them; the C++ agrees, CRC included).
**Alternatives:** keep 0.125 (the bias stays, 7.7 degrees on a slowly turning vehicle); 1/128 (best on one flight, but a platform move at 300 dps and the campaign's injected jumps would saturate it); a 24-bit or a wider frame (a protocol redesign: three 24-bit values do not fit six data bytes); leave the bus and correct the bias in the estimator (the estimator is the next item and needs this anyway: it cannot recover what the bus has already thrown away).
**Consequences:** 631 C++ tests pass; the fault campaign (12,362 scenarios, strict) has no anomaly and its curves moved with the finer sensor; four tests and one pinned hash changed because the quantity they pin changed (the fuzz range, the golden frames, the saturating-gyro values, the digest of the old fault kinds' traffic), and two closed-loop tests now use 2 % frame loss because **the flight lost at 1 % loss in TS-16 survives it now, without the resync** (4 of 32 patterns lost at 1 %, 23 of 32 at 5 %): the divergence between the replicas is as real as before and the resync stays, but part of what was measured as a lost flight was the coarse gyro's bias (`TRADE_STUDIES.md` TS-16 and TS-23 carry both measurements). A computer on the old scale among new ones would be voted out as faulty. The rig's IMU scaling reads the constant, so it follows.
**Revisit:** if the real IMU's noise at 0.031 dps per count (its own resolution is about 0.009) makes the votes chatter on the bench, or if a vehicle's rate exceeds 1024 dps legitimately (a spin-stabilised stage; the platform cannot do it).

## ADR-033 (accepted 7 Oct 2026): The vehicle's dynamics go beyond a rigid body on a first-order servo: each an option of the vehicle file
**Context:** the owner asked on 6 Oct 2026 for a full simulator that would work for any rocket or spacecraft, "everything that you can think of". ADR-031 made the vehicle data and listed what was still missing (`VEHICLE_SPEC.md` section 8): the actuator, the exhaust, the separation, the roll, the propellant and the structure. The flight software steers two tilt planes through a servo it assumes ideal; whether it survives a plant that is not one is the question the write-up has to answer.
**Decision:** seven options of the vehicle file, **all off by default so that every flight and every documented number is bit for bit what it was**: (1) a second-order gimbal servo (natural frequency, damping) with the rate limit on its speed and the travel limit that stops it; (2) backlash, the play between the servo and the engine; (3) jet damping, `-md r x (omega x r)` per engine; (4) the push (`separation_dv_ms`) and the tip-off rates of a separation; (5) a roll controller of the vehicle's own, with the roll angle as a new state; (6) the first slosh mode of each tank (Abramson/Dodge: mass, frequency and place from the liquid's depth and the tank's radius), taken out of the rigid body and put back as a force, the sloshing mass moving relative to the tank; (7) one bending mode with its modal coordinate in each plane, driven by the lateral thrust, bending the thrust and read by the vehicle's gyros and accelerometers. An example, `vehicles/launcher_dynamics.json`, turns them all on for the two-stage launcher and is flown by the real flight software.
**Why:** each is a standard piece of a launch-vehicle model and each can break the loop of a controller designed for a rigid body on an ideal servo. Measured on the example: the real flight software holds 0.28 degree rms through all of it at the example's numbers, and **loses the flight when the slope of the bending mode at the IMU reaches 1 per metre of one sign or 0.5 of the other** (or the mode moves to 8 Hz with 0.7): the flight software has no filter for a mode (`DYNAMICS.md` section 9). That is the kind of result the simulator is for, and it needs the model to be a real one: the first versions of the slosh coupling gave a launcher that accelerated 26 % faster (the sloshing mass had been taken out of the axial acceleration too, and the unit tests, which looked at the lateral motion, had not seen it; flying the real vehicle did), and the first roll controller was put on a stage that separates and the flight was lost after the separation, which is the physics and not a bug (a roll controller belongs to a stage that stays).
**Alternatives:** keep the rigid body and state the limit (the write-up could not say anything about a vehicle's bending or slosh); a full finite-element structure and CFD slosh (the data does not exist for a made-up vehicle, and the simulator is not for design); the slosh as a pendulum with the force at the pivot (the same dynamics; the spring-mass form was kept for the arithmetic, the bob placed inside the liquid for the shallow case); more than one bending mode or slosh mode (the first is the one that couples; another would double the assumed numbers without adding a mechanism).
**Consequences:** the simulator's state has the roll angle, the slosh displacements and rates, and the bending mode (all zero and unused by default); every number of the dynamics in an example is an assumption, said so in the file and on `DYNAMICS.md`; 667 C++ tests, 481 simulator mutants today (the 464 of its day, three added by ADR-034 and fourteen by ADR-035) (the first run of each group left survivors, all closed by tests or documented as equivalent); the flight software does not change. The cost is in the slosh and the bending: a few percent of the speed of a vehicle that uses them.
**Revisit:** when a servo, a tank or a structure is measured; if the write-up claims anything about bending, add the notch filter or the gain-stabilised design to the flight software (a core change, with its own ADR).

## ADR-034 (accepted 8 Oct 2026): A web console for the operator: one page that shows the system and sends the commands, in Python's standard library, local, and a client of the bus
**Context:** the owner asked on 8 Oct 2026, with the software side finished and the hardware arriving the next day, for "some sort of UI so I can see all of this", behaving like a flight console with the launch sequence, how the voting works, what is happening, a small simulation overview of where the rocket is, the vehicle's parameters, and the operator's commands (launch, arm, reintegrate), "a self hosted website or other if you think there is something better". The system was seen through a dozen terminals (each node's console, `tfc_peers listen`, `launch`, `command`, the simulator's log, a CSV for a plot), and the reasons for what the nodes do (why a node was latched) are on their consoles, not on the bus.
**Decision:** a local web application: `console/tfc-console` serves one page of ten tabs (Mission, Launch, Voting, Flight, Commands, Faults, Vehicle, Rig, Bus, Events). The server is **Python's standard library only** (`http.server`, SocketCAN as in `tfc_peers/bus.py`) and the page is plain ES modules and canvas, no framework and nothing fetched from the network. It reads the flight bus and the nodes' consoles (a process the console started, or a serial port), decodes with the same code the virtual peers use, and sends only **authenticated ground commands** (the frames `tfc_peers command` sends, with the same counter file), two-step where the protocol says so and only after the operator confirms. It listens on 127.0.0.1, every API request needs a session token, and the page carries a content-security policy. It starts and breaks the virtual rig's processes, injects faults through `tfc_peers run --control` (new: one command per line on standard input, applied between frames) and measures their detection, validates and previews vehicles with `tfc_fly`, records a session as the bus plus a sidecar of the consoles and the simulator's telemetry, and replays it through the same model. Two small, tested additions to the simulator make "where the rocket is" real: `Vehicle6::range()` (the arc along the surface from the launch point, in `tfc_fly`'s CSV as `range_m`) and `tfc_simd --telemetry PORT` (the simulator's own state, 10 times a second, as JSON on UDP).
**Why:** (1) the operator's questions are about *several* things at once (is it go, who is voting, what did the node say, where is the vehicle), and a page that puts the bus, the consoles and the truth side by side answers them where terminals cannot. (2) A browser is on every machine, draws the charts and the diagrams, can be on a second monitor or a tablet, and needs nothing installed; the repository's tools already use only the standard library for the bus, and the bench has no guaranteed internet, so nothing is downloaded and a run is reproducible from the repository. (3) The console must not be a second source of truth about the flight: it shows what the computers say and labels what it computes (the go/no-go is the launch checklist's rule, held by a test to the same answer over 400 random states). (4) Putting the fault engine's control in the peers (`--control`) rather than the console keeps one fault engine (the one the campaign and its mutation testing cover) and keeps timing-critical frame generation out of the web server's process. (5) It was exercised against the real firmware before it was written down: a launch, a node killed in flight (detected 2 frames later, as the matrix says), and a 3 dps bias injected (2 frames, "vote disagreement"); the recorded run's max-Q (31.5 kPa at T+64.9 s) equals `tfc_fly`'s nominal flight to the digit.
**Alternatives:** a terminal UI (a TUI needs a library the machine does not have, cannot draw the trajectory and the charts well, and is not shareable on a second screen); a desktop toolkit (Qt or Tk: heavier, and the repository would then depend on it); Grafana, PlotJuggler or Foxglove (good for plots, none knows the launch sequence, the interlock or a ground command, and the console's job is the commands and the checklist as much as the plots); a framework front end (React or Vue with a bundler: a build step and a network of dependencies to defend in an interview, for a page that needs none); the console doing the fault injection itself (a second copy of the fault engine, in the wrong process); a console that decides (a go/no-go it invents, a health it computes): rejected, it would be a second, untested flight logic.
**Consequences:** about 5,600 lines of console (server, page and styles) and 2,400 of tests; the simulator gained a telemetry output and a CSV column (a 692-test C++ suite, three new simulator mutants all killed); `tfc_peers run` gained `--control`. The page is a development and bench tool, not flight software, and says what it does not claim (`design/CONSOLE.md` section 14). The rig it starts is sensitive to a loaded host like the live tests are (a headless browser start-up has cost a frame and a cascade of latches; a page left open has not). The ground key is still the public bench key (ADR-019): the page says so wherever a command is sent.
**Revisit:** when the hardware is on the bench (the serial paths have not met a board; the Pico and the supervisor may want different polling or commands), if a second operator ever matters (counters are serialised, not designed for two), and when a real uplink needs a real key.

## ADR-035 (accepted 9 Oct 2026): A 3D viewer: the vehicle seen as a vehicle, from a pose the simulator sends, on a page of its own, with the air drawn as what it is (an illustration of the simulator's numbers)
**Context:** the owner asked on 9 Oct 2026, with the console finished, for "a 3D view of the rocket" in place of the 2D box: "a real looking 3D version of the simulation that shows all the aspects of what is going on and everything that can be measured", the airflow, the pressure and the rest, as sub-views to look at one process in more detail, with the centre of pressure and the centre of mass in particular, a Starship-class model by default and a way to bring a CAD model of their own, procedural scenery (with a note of what could be downloaded), and "model driven is good now", true CFD later. They left open whether it belonged on the console's page or elsewhere "so it doesn't lag anything".
**Decision:** a page of its own, `console/web/viewer/`, served by the console's server and opened in its own window; WebGL through three.js (r170, vendored, MIT; the one external library, kept in the repository as the bench has no network); six lenses (Overview, Aerodynamics, Atmosphere, Propulsion, Loads, Attitude); a **pose** (`sim/vehicle/viewer_state.hpp`: position, attitude, the air, the forces and where they act, every engine, gimbal, fin and tank) that `tfc_simd --viewer PORT` sends 50 times a second over UDP and `tfc_fly --pose FILE` writes to a file, with a **spec** (the vehicle as read, the planet, the air by kilometre); the pose goes to the page on a stream of its own, so a console with no viewer open pays nothing for it. The sky, atmosphere and planet are one fragment shader and the vehicle and the launch site a second scene in metres about the centre of gravity (floats cannot hold both). The vehicle is generated from its vehicle file; `Starship` is a style (a stainless 9 m stack) and `vehicles/starship.json` a 33 + 6 engine vehicle for it to be, from public round numbers (not SpaceX data, flown by the real flight software to 435 km); a glTF file in `console/models/` replaces the generated body. The air is drawn from the model's numbers: the pressure on the skin (modified Newtonian), streamlines (slender-body potential flow), the bow shock (Taylor-Maccoll), and each says on the page that it is an illustration; the Loads lens is a lumped-mass estimate and says so.
**Why:** (1) a vehicle whose problem is its attitude, its air and its loads is best understood by seeing them where they are: the unstable vehicle held by its gimbals is a picture of a centre of pressure ahead of a centre of mass; the 33 engines of a booster are a map. (2) The rule of the project is that a number says where it came from, so the viewer has a column of *measured*, *derived* and *illustrative* for every lens (`design/VIEWER.md` section 6) and the simulator is not asked to be a CFD solver to supply a nicer picture. (3) The cost of a 3D view is the browser's and the GPU's: a second window is a second process, a second *server* would split the telemetry port and the token, and the rig, whose 10 ms deadlines a loaded host disturbs, is asked for nothing more than a UDP stream. (4) The flight computers carry the reference vehicle's tables, so a vehicle of any other kind can only be flown by `tfc_fly`; the viewer therefore has *Fly a vehicle* (it runs `tfc_fly` and plays the pose file), and every vehicle in `vehicles/` is seen at once. (5) Everything is checked against the real thing: a pose is held to the vehicle by C++ tests and fourteen mutants, a launch through the real firmware on `vcan0` arrives at 50 a second, and the old console recording (10 Hz telemetry only) still shows, as a generic vehicle with the missing quantities named.
**Alternatives:** the 3D view inside the console's page (one main thread for the charts, the rig's controls and a 60 Hz renderer; a stalled chart would be a stalled picture); a second server (above); a desktop application with a game engine (a large dependency and a build, for something that has to run from the repository with nothing installed); a real CFD solver behind the viewer (the right tool for the air but not for a live view, and the project has no solver or grid: `VIEWER.md` section 9 says where one would plug in); imagery of the Earth (the bench is offline; a procedural planet is honest about being one, and an imagery pack can replace it); a model downloaded from a model site (none with a licence the project could carry was found for this vehicle; the Starship's proportions are public and the glTF import is the way for a model that is the owner's).
**Consequences:** about 3,659 lines of page script and 95 of style, 389 of server, a 317-line C++ header and 986 lines of tests (the vendored three.js is 45,000 more and not ours); the simulator gained eleven read-only accessors and a `Loop` hook, `tfc_simd` two options and `tfc_fly` two (all output only; the flight and the campaign are unchanged); 14 new simulator mutants (481 in all), all killed; a test of the Starship-class vehicle flown through hot staging; a fifth vehicle file. The viewer is a bench and design tool and says so; its GPU cost is measured on one machine; its pictures have no automated check beyond the data they draw from (a mutant of a picture does not exist). Starting a headless browser during a live rig run is the same hazard as for the console.
**Revisit:** when a CFD case exists (the pressure map, the streamlines and the shock are the three seams); when the Starship-class vehicle is wanted on the rig (done by ADR-036: the console builds the images for the chosen vehicle); if the viewer is ever shown to someone on a machine with a weaker GPU (measure it, and the quality setting); and when the hardware is on the bench, if the platform's own tilt should be drawn beside the vehicle's.

## ADR-036 (accepted 9 Oct 2026): The rig flies the vehicle the operator chooses: the console builds the flight computers for it, from the real firmware, and the simulator flies the same vehicle with its own sensors
**Context:** the owner asked on 9 Oct 2026 to "pick which spacecraft to use" in the launch UI, have it "build that spacecraft on the rig", then start the launch and see "what is happening based on how the flight computers are reacting". Until then the rig could fly only the reference vehicle: the flight computers carry, compiled in, the pitch program and the gain schedule designed on it (`flight_tables.hpp`), and `tfc_simd --vehicle` could fly another vehicle only against the wrong tables (VEHICLE_SPEC.md said "regenerate them").
**Decision:** (1) choosing a vehicle on the Launch tab builds three flight-computer images with *that vehicle's* tables: `tfc_fly --check` (the simulator's reader refuses a bad file with its line and column), `tfc_gen_tables --vehicle FILE`, and three `west build`s of the unchanged application with two new switches, `-DTFC_TABLES_HEADER=<its tables>` (the tables the application includes) and `CONFIG_TFC_ESTIMATOR_NO_ACCEL` (the attitude estimator does not fuse the accelerometer: a vehicle under thrust reads no gravity, SIM_FIDELITY.md 3.2). Both default to what the firmware always did; nothing of the flight code changes. (2) The tables are designed with only the vehicle's **design** knobs applied; the **plant** knobs go to the simulator alone (departures the computers must not know). (3) The images live in `build/vehicles/NAME-KEY/`, KEY a hash of the design file and the firmware's sources. (4) The simulator is given the vehicle file and `--vehicle-true` (its own sensors: a platform that tilts a few degrees cannot follow a vehicle that pitches past 45°). (5) The reference vehicle's images stay the committed build; the reference's computers are never started on another vehicle. (6) The rig must be stopped to change vehicle. (7) `tfc_simd`'s limit on a SYNC frame number rises from 30,000 (five minutes) to 100,000, because a Starship-class ascent is about 470 s of flight after the pad and the countdown.
**Why:** (1) the thing the owner wants to see is the *real* flight computers reacting to a vehicle, so the images must be the real firmware with that vehicle's design, not a software stand-in; the build is ten seconds an image, so doing it when asked costs less than keeping a build for every vehicle. (2) A knob that is a departure from the design, given to the tables, would be a flight computer that knew the thrust was 10 % low: the test of robustness would be void. (3) A cache keyed by the design and the firmware cannot serve a stale image after either changes. (4) The estimator without the accelerometer is what the closed-loop examples already use (`tfc_sens`, `tfc_fly`), so the rig and the preview agree. (5) An unstable plant that the design cannot stabilise fails in the generator, which is the honest place for it.
**Alternatives:** a software flight function in the console (a stand-in that is not the firmware: rejected, it would show what a model of the computers does); one image with the tables loaded at run time over the bus (a change of the flight code's data path and of its certification story, for a convenience); building every example vehicle ahead of time (a build for each, stale when the firmware changes); allowing a change of vehicle while the rig runs (the running images have the old tables).
**Consequences:** the console gained `rigbuild.py` (about 230 lines), two routes, a card on the Launch tab and 14 tests (13 in `test_console_rigbuild.py`, with stand-ins for `west`, the generator and the reader, and one in `test_console_vehicles.py`); the firmware application gained two build switches. **Measured, 9 Oct 2026, live on vcan0, with the Block 2 class file of that day (ADR-037 replaced it):** the Starship-class vehicle chosen through `POST /api/rig/vehicle`, built (check, tables of 2,426 bytes, three images: about half a minute), the rig started, all three computers calibrated, launched through the real ARM and EXECUTE: all three healthy and Triplex to the end, burn-out at about T+470 s at 489 km and 7.7 km/s, the tilt 0.64° from the program's (72.64° against 72.0°, the one sample taken there), the stack's mass falling from 1,841 t to 1,393 t between T+155 s and T+170 s (the staging), the viewer receiving 50 poses a second from the rig's simulator. **Measured 10 Oct 2026, every other example vehicle through the same route** (choose, build, start the rig, ARM and EXECUTE, sampled every second; all three computers healthy and in Triplex to the end each time): the sounding rocket to 68.7 km and 1,127 m/s in 60 s (worst pitch error 1.79°; `tfc_fly`: 68.6 km, 1,128 m/s); the reference with a design knob changed (`design.gains.wn` 3.5) to 31.9 km and 994 m/s at 100 s (0.11°); the spacecraft in orbit, 500 km and 7,613 m/s, slewed through 30° (0.29°); the two-stage launcher to 489.1 km and 3,276 m/s at 400 s (0.47°; `tfc_fly`: 486.6 km, 3,281 m/s); the launcher with dynamics to 488.7 km and 3,282 m/s (2.15°). The V3 file of ADR-037: 0.66° worst, 227 km and 7.54 km/s at T+495 s. Not measured: a vehicle whose design the generator cannot close; a vehicle with a flight longer than the mission frame's 645 s; any of this on a board.
**Revisit:** when a vehicle's flight exceeds the 645 s the mission frame's 16 bits count (SYNC's mission frame is a 16-bit count); when a second operator shares the console (two choices of vehicle); when the real flight computers (not native_sim) must be flashed with a vehicle's tables, which is a different build and a release step, not a button.

## ADR-037 (accepted 9 Oct 2026): The Starship example is a V3 (Block 3) class vehicle, from reported numbers and stated estimates, and the viewer's Starship has panel-adjustable details
**Context:** the owner asked on 9 Oct 2026 for the Starship to be "as close to real life as possible v3" and adjustable. `vehicles/starship.json` was a Block 2 class vehicle from round numbers (2.26 MN engines, 123 m, four grid fins). Starship V3 flew on 22 May 2026 (Flight 12), which is after most of what I know, so the numbers were searched on 9 Oct 2026.
**Decision:** (1) `vehicles/starship.json` takes the reported V3 numbers: 124.4 m (72.3 m booster, 52.1 m ship), 33 + 6 Raptor 3 of 280 tf (2.746 MN at sea level), 3,400 t and 1,600 t of propellant, Flight 12's 37.5 t payload, hot staging a little after T+142 s (the booster burns out at about 141.5 s, the ship lights at 142.4 s, the booster is let go at 143.5 s); and states in the file and in `VIEWER.md` section 7, number by number, which are reported and which are my estimates (the dry masses, the vacuum engine's thrust, the pitch program, the ship's throttle). (2) The ship's engines cut off at 491 s (the flight's planned SECO) at 85 % throttle, so that the ship ends with propellant left, as the real one does. (3) The pitch program now ends at 85° from the vertical, so the trajectory is a near-horizontal suborbital one like the flight's, not the 72° steep ascent of the earlier file. (4) The viewer draws three grid fins (50 % larger in area, set lower) and offers the Block 2 look and every detail (the fins' number, size and place, the flaps, the ring, the catch pins) as sliders in the Overview lens; they are the picture's and change nothing about the flight.
**Why:** (1) the owner asked for it, and a vehicle that states what is public and what is mine is more use than one that looks real and hides its estimates. (2) A ship that burns all its propellant would reach 8.4 km/s and an orbit the real flight did not fly: the reserve is what made the numbers consistent. (3) The 85° program was flown through the real firmware (Launch tab, `vcan0`) before it was adopted: the flight computers hold it. (4) The grid fins are not in the simulator's aerodynamics, so making them a picture's detail, with that said, keeps the physics honest.
**Alternatives:** keep Block 2 and add a second file (the owner asked for V3; the old numbers are in the history); give the vehicle file fields for grid fins and flaps (a change of the reader, the C++ spec and the mutation set for a drawing); match Flight 12's trajectory exactly by tuning (the real guidance and masses are not public; a tuned fit would claim more than the data allows).
**Consequences:** one C++ test changed its numbers (mass, max-Q window); the demo flight `console/demo/starship-ascent.pose.jsonl.gz` was regenerated (2.2 MB); the viewer's overview gained the Starship panel. **Measured:** `tfc_fly`, vehicle sensors: max-Q 43.5 kPa at 49.0 s (Flight 12: T+45 s), booster burn-out about 141.5 s (MECO 142 s), separation at 143.5 s at 75 km and 2.40 km/s, cut-off at 491 s at 231 km and 7.50 km/s, with a 15 s pad phase (the attitude error 0.57° rms, 1.8° at worst) (Flight 12: 195 km apogee, about 7.4 km/s: the same kind, not a match); through the rig (10 Oct 2026, sampled every second), all three computers healthy to the end, the pitch tilt within 0.66° of the program at every sample, 7.54 km/s at 227 km at T+495 s. The first version of the file lit the ship after the booster had already gone (no hot staging, against the ADR's own words) and its test asserted a wrong lift-off mass: the pull request's CI caught both, and they were fixed before this ADR's numbers were re-measured. Not measured: anything about the real vehicle's aerodynamics, structure or gimbal limits; `tfc_fly` flown with a pad phase of only 3 s (as my first runs were) shows the yaw drifting to 10° because the gyro bias is not calibrated out: that was misread, in the first version of this ADR and of the file's header, as a property of the tilt angles near the horizontal, and the program was stopped at 72° on that account in the earlier Block 2 file; with the 15 s pad the flight to 85° holds within 1.8°.
**Revisit:** when SpaceX publishes V3 masses (replace the estimates); when the ship's entry is wanted (the simulator has no re-entry); when a CAD model of the V3 is available (import it, `console/models/README.md`).

## ADR-038 (accepted 9 Oct 2026): Earth imagery for the viewer is an optional pack downloaded from NASA, never committed, with the procedural planet as the fallback
**Context:** the owner asked on 9 Oct 2026 to "add Earth imagery, see what looks and performs the best". The viewer's planet was procedural (a made-up coast and continents) because it had no imagery to use offline, and the bench has no internet; the owner's machine does.
**Decision:** `console/tfc-imagery` downloads a pack of about 13 MB into `console/imagery/` (git-ignored) from NASA GIBS, whose imagery is public domain and needs no key: Blue Marble Next Generation at 4K and 8K, Black Marble for the night side, and, for a launch site in the United States, Landsat WELD patches of 4° and 1° round the pad (108 m and 27 m a pixel). A manifest says what each file is, where it is and where it came from; the console serves only the files the manifest names; the viewer's sky shader puts the pictures on the planet by longitude and latitude (the pad is placed at the known launch site nearest in latitude to the simulator's launch point), layers the scales, keeps the picture's colours, lights the sea with its own sun glint and draws the atmosphere and clouds over it. Without a pack, or on choosing *Procedural*, nothing changes. The choice (best there is, procedural, 4K, 8K, each with or without the launch site, and the night lights) is in the Overview lens. The near scene's flat ground gives way to the picture beyond a few hundred metres of the pad.
**Why:** (1) the planet is the largest thing in most frames and the most obviously made up; a real coast under the vehicle is what makes a flight look like a flight. (2) A pack that is downloaded, not committed, keeps the repository small, keeps the licence question out of it (NASA imagery is public domain; a third-party tile service's terms are not) and keeps the viewer working offline. (3) Landsat WELD is the only 30 m source on the same service and is public domain; a 27 m pixel is its own resolution. (4) Cloud-free Blue Marble goes under the viewer's own clouds; a picture with weather in it would show the clouds twice.
**Alternatives:** commit a 4K Earth texture (14 MB of binary history for something every user can fetch); a tile pyramid and a tile service (the right answer for a worldwide, many-zoom viewer: a loader, a cache, a licence and a network dependency, for a viewer that flies from one pad); Sentinel-2 cloudless from a third party (sharper, 10 m, but its licence is non-commercial and it is not NASA's); elevation (a height map and a tessellated planet: not done, the pictures are of the ground and not terrain).
**Consequences:** `tfc_console/imagery.py` (fetch, manifest, pack), two routes, 9 tests (`test_console_imagery.py`, the network a stand-in), a shader that samples up to four pictures with explicit gradients, and `?bench=1`. **Measured** (development GPU, 1280 x 720, Overview lens): the procedural planet 6 ms median (9 ms p95) at 600 km, 7 ms (11) at 15 km and 5 ms (8) on the pad; every picture choice 4 to 5 ms (5 to 8 p95): the pictures are not slower than the noise they replace. GPU memory is what grows: about 400 MB for the most detailed choice (computed, not measured). Load (fetch and decode) 50 to 70 ms a choice on localhost (bounded by a 50 ms poll). **Not measured**: the GPU upload as a cost of its own, a network fetch, another GPU or browser. The pictures are the year 2000's over the United States and a monthly composite elsewhere; there is no terrain.
**Revisit:** when a second site or a worldwide flight is wanted (tiles, then); when the memory is a problem on a laptop (a 4K default); when terrain matters (a height map, and then the near ground can follow it); when a second GPU or browser is available to measure on.

## ADR-039 (accepted 10 Oct 2026): The flight computers fly a whole mission: navigation, guidance to orbit, a quaternion attitude loop and a stage's return, from tables a design run makes, in deterministic double precision, proved on the host and not yet on the board
**Context:** the owner asked on 9 and 10 Oct 2026 for "the booster boost-back and landing/catch for only the spacecraft that have multiple stages" ("some smaller rockets are just the rocket and they go back down"), "orbital insertion", "a guidance and navigation system", "a full aerodynamic system considering all the shapes", and real Starship numbers from the web, "once everything is green, merge and squash". Until then the flight computers held a pitch program with two tilt angles (good to about 80 degrees from the vertical), did not know where the vehicle was, could not command an engine or a surface, and the simulator had one body, subsonic-to-Mach-3 aerodynamics and no ground.
**Decision:** (1) A **mission** is a table of phases in the vehicle file (hold, program, PEG burn, coast, boost-back, glide, landing, chute, done; each with its engine groups, events, mixer, reference slew rate and end condition), turned by `mission_build.hpp` into constant tables; the computers carry the tables and the sequencer (`mission.hpp`) moves through them. (2) **Navigation** is strapdown in a launch-centred inertial frame with velocity Verlet, a fixed-gain GNSS observer with a gate, and the attitude corrected under thrust from the GNSS velocity innovation across the specific force; the attitude estimator's accelerometer correction is switched off in flight. (3) **Guidance to orbit** is a numerical predictor-corrector in the family of PEG (five unknowns, Levenberg-Marquardt, one prediction per call), seeded from the design run, with the engines' decay impulse taken off the target. (4) **Attitude** is a quaternion PD-I loop whose error is a rotation, with gains designed from the plant's own probes, per-point allocation to the surfaces, and the gimbal command scaled by the thrust the gains were made at. (5) **A stage's return** is boost-back to a predicted impact point (with the air, measured by the design run), entry steered by lift, and a landing burn that follows a speed profile to a point under the arms, with the catch height following the mass. (6) The plant gained engines driven by group, surfaces, parachutes, separation that spawns a vehicle of its own, a ground, a tower, and aerodynamics over the whole speed range (Newtonian impact theory, plates, grid fins). (7) All of it is `double` built from `+ - * /` and `sqrt` (`dmath.hpp`), three replicas computing the same bits. (8) The host tool `tfc_mission` designs and flies a vehicle file's mission; CI runs it on the two committed missions.
**Why:** (1) the owner's goal is a flight computer that does what a real launcher's does, and a booster's return and an orbital insertion are the two things it could not do at all; a pitch program cannot be made to do either by adding gains. (2) Tables, not code, per vehicle: the same sequencer flies a sounding rocket's parachute descent and a booster's catch, and a design tool that probes the plant removes the hand-tuning that would otherwise be per phase (it did not remove all of it, `GNC.md` section 11). (3) Double precision in software: the guidance integrates trajectories over minutes and single precision was not enough for the orbit (the closed-form PEG also diverged on the ship's case, which is why it is numerical). (4) The order the problems were found in is in `RECOVERY.md` section 6: nine reasons in a row why a booster that caught in the ideal mode crashed in the closed loop, each a thing a simpler design would have hidden.
**Alternatives:** keep the pitch program and add a "return" script (it would have been another vehicle-specific table with no navigation to check it against); closed-form PEG (diverged on the ship; kept as a test); a Kalman filter for the navigation (a covariance and bias states the project has no data to tune; the fixed-gain observer plus the attitude aid meets the catch's tolerance on the host, and a filter is the first thing to add if the board's IMU needs it); a second language or a flight-code generator for the guidance (the project's rule is one C++ core tested on the host).
**Consequences:** about 4,600 lines of core code (`dmath`, `nav`, `peg`, `attitude`, `descent`, `mission` and the parts of `flight`, `estimator`, `protocol`), 6,200 lines of plant and design tools, 3,300 of tests, two vehicle files, one host tool and three documents; the flight code's coverage gate stays at 100 % of lines and 98 % of branches, the simulator's at 99 % and 90 %. The catch is marginal (32 of 36 noise seeds, GNSS accuracy of 3 m or better), the ship's orbit is 234 x 257 km for a target of 250, and the ship does not come back. **No timing on the board, no firmware path, no rig, console or viewer integration** (`GNC.md` section 11).
**Revisit:** when the guidance is timed on the Cortex-M4F (it may need a lower rate or a smaller predictor); when the firmware carries a mission (the frames of `PROTOCOL.md`, the tables in the image, `tfc_gen_tables`); when the ship's entry is wanted; when a differential GNSS model or a second receiver is added; when winds and engine failures are put into the booster's return (the catch is the first thing they will break).

## ADR-040 (accepted 11 Oct 2026): While the virtual rig runs, the rest of the user's programs are kept off the CPUs it runs on
**Context:** a Starship flight on the rig, with the 3D viewer open in a browser, latched nodes A and B out and the system went to safe. The flight computers did what they are built to do, so the question was what made two of them late. Measured on the development host (an i5-10600KF, 6 cores and 12 threads, Linux 7.0, no root, `ulimit -r` 0): a whole 472 s flight with no browser stayed in Triplex with one one-frame ACT blip; starting Firefox with the viewer and the console on an idle rig latched B and C before launch; twelve busy loops for four seconds latched the nodes (A voted out by B and C for "vote disagreement", then "sequence error", then Simplex or Safe) whether the loops were at nice 0 or nice 19, and whether the rig's cgroup had CPU weight 10 000 against the load's 1; six busy loops did nothing; and the rig's threads spent 0.5 to 1.8 s of those four seconds waiting for a CPU (`/proc/PID/schedstat`) although each process uses about 5 % of one. A `native_sim` node hands control between its threads about 14 000 times a second, and every hand-off is a Linux wake-up, so a host with no idle CPU delays the frame by more than the 10 ms it has. A 1 ms timer in the same conditions was woken at most 3.6 ms late (0.3 ms inside the heavy cgroup): the rig is far more exposed than that, which is why the weight alone did not help.
**Decision:** the console, by default, (1) pins the rig's processes to a reserved set of CPUs, the hyper-threads of the first half of the physical cores (CPUs 0-2 and 6-8 here), and (2) moves the other processes of the same user, thread by thread, to the remaining CPUs for as long as the rig runs, remembering each one's mask and putting it back when the rig stops, when the switch is turned off or when the console exits; a sweep every 3 s catches what started in between, a process left confined to the others by a console that was killed is freed by the next (a pid file says whose it was), and a host with fewer than 8 logical CPUs or 4 cores gets no isolation and a reason on the Rig tab. `--no-isolate` and the Rig tab's switch turn it off. Nothing of other users' or the kernel's is touched, and nothing needs a privilege.
**Verification:** the same twelve-loop burn that latched the nodes four times left them in Triplex with the isolation on (102 processes, 899 threads moved); Firefox started with the viewer and the console while the rig was in Triplex did not latch anything; 14 unit tests move and restore real processes; the rig test checks that its children really start on the reserved CPUs.
**Alternatives:** real-time scheduling (`SCHED_FIFO` for the rig) is the usual remedy and needs `rtprio` in `limits.d` and a new login or a capability; not done because it cannot be tested here without root, and it is the better answer on a dedicated machine (the Rig tab would show it as available); nice values and cgroup CPU weight (measured not to help); pinning the rig alone (measured not to help while the load can still run on the same CPUs); asking the user to close other programs (what the docs said, and what a viewer that is part of the tool should not need); fewer thread hand-offs in the firmware's `native_sim` build (a change to the flight image for the sake of the desktop rig, and the board has none of it).
**Consequences:** the user's desktop has six logical CPUs while a rig runs on a twelve-thread host, and a process that was deliberately confined to exactly the rig's CPUs is freed at the end. The cure is for this host and this kind of load: it does not make the rig immune to a stall from the kernel, the GPU driver or another user, and the rig is still not a hard real-time system (`LIMITATIONS.md`). The HIL rig on the Nucleos has none of this problem and is where the timing is to be believed.
**Revisit:** on the first run on another machine; if a flight latches again with the isolation on (the Events tab and `/proc/PID/schedstat` are the first places to look); if real-time scheduling can be tested.

## ADR-041 (accepted 11 Oct 2026): The 3D viewer shows less by default, aims its cameras at the vehicle, colours the flow, and draws the ship's flaps from the simulator's own surface table
**Context:** the owner, looking at the viewer on a live Starship flight, said that some lenses were very cluttered, that the UI was buggy in some views (the ground view first), that the aerodynamics were "just some straight white lines", and that the ship's flaps did not show or looked bad: they should be flush with the fuselage, have a better shape, and change the aerodynamics. Looking at the pictures: two 344 px docks of open cards covered about 40 % of the window; the aerodynamics lens drew a translucent cone, 150 parallel streamlines, six force labels and the pressure map together; the ground camera looked in a fixed direction (the vehicle was behind a panel, or a dot with daytime stars round it, and at 14 km a blob because a 0.3 m near plane leaves a 24-bit depth buffer 40 m of resolution there); the wide camera showed empty sky from T+100 s; the charts had axes like 73.5M, 73.5M, 73.5M; and the flaps were four dark rectangles lying on a dark skin that appeared only in the viewer (the vehicle file had none, so they changed nothing).
**Decision:** (1) cards fold and remember it, reference and later charts start folded, docks hide from the edge; (2) the cameras are a bar on every lens; the ground camera looks at the vehicle with an adaptive field of view and a free-look offset, the near plane follows the distance, the wide camera frames the pad and the vehicle together, a vehicle too small to see is circled; (3) each view of the aerodynamics tells one story and its own switches are the only ones shown; labels are pushed apart; the shock is a thin rim; (4) one full-spectrum colour scale, ribbon streamlines released in rings ahead of the nose and coloured by the skin pressure beneath them; (5) the ship's four flaps are `surfaces` of `vehicles/starship.json` (the simulator's articulated-surface model already existed for the booster's grid fins), the pose carries each surface's deflection (`surf`, `sdep`), the viewer draws them from that table as curved steel panels flush with the hull that swing out on their hinge to the reported angle, and the plate model's friction term for a flap is scaled by its exposure (zero flush, full by 10 degrees), so that a flush flap changes no flight and a deployed one changes the forces and the moment; the plant does not evaluate a flap lying flush at all (under 0.05 degrees), which is the same statement and keeps four flaps from costing every Starship step 40 % more (the first version did, and the sil job ran out of its 30 minutes).
**Verification:** C++: the stowed-flap test now says a flush flap carries nothing and a standing one its friction; a new test that a flap 40 degrees out carries 50 times the load of a flush one at another place; a test that the Starship file has four flush flaps on the ship, that the viewer's spec has them and that every pose carries `surf` (803 tests, 0 failed). The Starship ascent is unchanged, bit for bit in the loads (a test flies the file with and without its flaps for 60 s and compares the aerodynamic force and moment): 230.8 km and 7,496 m/s at the end, max-Q 43.5 kPa at 49.0 s, attitude error 0.569 deg rms. The in-page selftest: 52 of 52, including that the vehicle is inside the picture for the orbit, chase, track, ground and wide cameras and that a flap turns with the pose.
**Alternatives:** make the flaps a picture choice with an angle slider (the first attempt: it changed nothing in the physics and said the opposite of the plant); put their loads into the Barrowman fin build-up (the plate model of the surfaces already exists and is the one the grid fins use); WebGL `logarithmicDepthBuffer` for the far cameras (it disables early depth tests for the whole scene and fights the sky pass); a CFD-like flow field for the streamlines (section 9: a solver can replace them later).
**Consequences:** `vehicles/starship.json` gained four surfaces; every Starship flight through the plant now evaluates four more plate loads per step (zero while flush); `viewer_state.hpp` writes two more keys when a vehicle has surfaces; recorded pose files from before have no `surf` and draw the flaps flush. **Not done / not claimed:** the ship's flaps are not deployed by any mission (the ship does not come back, `GNC.md` section 11), so the only flights in which they move are those a mission file builds; the sizes and places are estimates; the aerodynamics lens's centre of pressure still comes from the body build-up and does not include the surfaces' loads; the streamlines' colour is a proxy (above), and the GPU cost of the ribbons was not measured.
**Revisit:** when a mission deploys the ship's flaps (the lens should then show their loads in the CP and the forces); when real flap dimensions can be sourced; if the ribbons cost frames on a laptop GPU.

## ADR-042 (accepted 11 Oct 2026): The viewer measures only what is still on the vehicle, follows a stage that returns as a vehicle of its own, and the booster is caught on a second pad
**Context:** the owner: "when a stage separates it still calculates like that stage is there", "stage separation should be smoother, see how they usually separate the stage, and then have the booster land again on another pad", and the flaps "just flush with the fuselage ... it doesn't look like a flap at all". Looking: every lens used the spec's whole length and diameter; the attitude ghost, the exhaust trail, the camera target and the loads' stations were the stack's after the booster was gone; the scene's origin (the centre of mass) jumped on a separation and the camera with it; the stage that was let go was a tumbling copy with a ballistic path, although in a mission world the simulator flies it (flip, boost-back, entry, landing burn, catch) under its own computers; the mission put the catch tower 45 m from the launch mount, so the picture showed the booster coming down on the launch mount.
**Decision:** (1) `Spec.extent(stg, pay)` is the one place that says what is still there, and every lens, the camera, the trail, the locator and the Reynolds number use it; the attitude ghost is per stage and hides with it; the camera compensates for the origin's jump and eases for 2.5 s. (2) The pose may carry `others`, the whole poses of the other bodies of a mission world; the viewer draws each as a vehicle of its own, a **Follow** bar turns the lenses and cameras to one, and a stage that is followed by the simulator is not drawn as debris. (3) `tfc_mission --pose-others` writes them, the viewer's source menu runs a mission, and the pose carries `cgt` (held by the arms) and `lnd` (standing). (4) `starship_return.json` puts the catch pad 400 m crosswind of the launch mount; the viewer draws a second pad there. (5) The flaps: a standing-off steel frame round a black tile face with a hinge barrel and brackets, 0.6 m thick, 0.22 m off the skin.
**Verification:** the in-page selftest, 60 of 60, on a real two-body mission flight at T+150 s: the other body is in the scene; the lenses measure 52.1 m of the 124.4 m; the catch pad exists; following the booster gives that stage's own spec, 39 engines and the vehicle inside the picture; following the main vehicle again restores it; the cameras show the vehicle inside the picture. The mission is caught on 7 of 8 seeds at 400 m and 7 of 8 at 45 m (the same seed fails both), the ship's orbit is 234 x 257 km; 805 C++ tests, 0 failed (a new one for `others`, `cgt` and `lnd`); the viewer service's tests include a stand-in `tfc_mission` that checks the arguments and a run of the real one on `rocket_recovery`.
**Alternatives:** make the debris copy smoother (a prettier lie: the simulator already flies the stage); put the booster in the main pose stream of the rig (the live rig flies the stack, not the return: a firmware and rig change that `GNC.md` section 11 lists as owed); put the second pad where it is in the real world (a few hundred metres, as chosen, is of the order of the Starbase's two towers' separation; the number is mine); freeze the camera at a separation instead of easing it (a cut is what the owner objected to).
**Consequences:** the viewer's frame loop has a body that is followed, a spec of that body and the main one; `world.js` keeps a map of the other bodies' models and plumes; the pad can have a second pad in its own frame; `data.js` interpolates the other bodies between two poses by name; a pose file with `others` is about 1.7 times as large. **Not done:** the ship does not return, so no flap deploys in any flight; no hot-stage ring as a separate part; no live rig for the returning stage.
**Revisit:** when the ship's entry is flown (the flaps, the tiles' heating on the 3D ship, a second catch); when the firmware carries a mission (the rig then shows the return live).

## ADR-043 (accepted 11 Oct 2026): The ship's flaps are hinged along the axis and stand out in the climb; the booster's grid fins come from the file and deploy for the return; the arms take the stage at the pins
**Context:** the owner: the main Starship has two fins at the top and two bigger at the bottom, extended when launching, "they are just structural but you can check"; and the booster, when it lands, has the fins out and the pad catches it at the top. What was there: flaps that lay on the skin (a speed brake's hinge: a tangent), grid fins that were a viewer-only dress, stowed for ever, arms of a tower that did not reach the pins. A search found no official statement and no dimensions; sources say the flaps are hinged and moved independently (SpaceX's own description quoted on a forum; a handout says the hinges are sealed under aero covers), that the real ones sit edge-on to the airflow in the climb and have flown fully extended (a forum post, unsourced), and that the V3 booster has three fins set low and integrated hot staging (so there is no jettisoned ring to draw: the item I had listed as owed is not one).
**Decision:** (1) `SurfaceSpec.axial_hinge`: a flap hinged along the axis, deflection 0 on the skin to 90 straight out, with a plate load that counts only the cross-flow's dynamic pressure and the friction of both faces, so that standing out in the climb it is edge-on and costs about 6 % more drag; the Starship's four flaps are that, stowed at 90, positions and sizes my estimates. (2) The booster's three grid fins are `surfaces` of `starship.json` as of the mission file, undeployed until the return, placed in a T (one on the spine, one on each flank 96 degrees round) on mounting pods that carry the flank catch pins (the owner's description; I found no source that settled whether V3's catch uses the fins or separate points, so the fins are the pins here and the file's `catch_pin_x_m` is their height); the viewer draws surfaces and no longer a dress. (3) The viewer folds and deploys grid fins from the pose's `sdep` bit over 1.5 s and draws the pins and the arms from the file's numbers; the arms close when the pose says the stage is held. (4) The entry's aim bias is 625 m and the landing's aim below the arms 1.8 m (the middles of plateaus measured on 20 seeds each, redone when the fins moved to the T): 78 of 80 caught on seeds that played no part in choosing them. (5) The plant caches each surface's frame and point.
**Verification:** C++ tests (807): the axial plate is edge-on to the axis and broadside across it, turns about its hinge from the skin to straight out with its load at its middle, a stream a few degrees off an edge-on plate gives about its friction and not a wing's force; the Starship file has three folded fins and four flaps at 90 degrees with the right mask, and a 25 s climb with and without them differs by friction only (drag up, under 15 %, no lateral force); the in-page selftest; pictures in a real browser. Ascent: 230.8 km, 7,495 m/s, 0.574 degrees rms.
**Alternatives:** keep the speed-brake hinge and draw the flaps differently from how they act (a picture that lies about the plant); draw them extended and give the plant a flush flap (the same lie); leave the booster's fins as a dress (they would never deploy).
**Consequences:** the Starship simulator takes 4.6 s to start (3.7 s without flaps), so the rig waits for it (the start order of ADR-040's round); a ship flap is evaluated in every step of every body that has the ship; the `starship.json` flight changes by flap friction; the `sil` job's timeout is 40 minutes (30 ran out once). **Not done:** the ship's own entry (flaps commanded in a belly flop) is the next ADR or not at all; see `GNC.md` section 11.
**Revisit:** when the ship's entry is flown; when real dimensions can be found; when a flap's hinge fairings and actuators are wanted as parts.

## ADR-044 (accepted 11 Oct 2026): The ship's way home is built as far as it goes and kept out of the mission that is verified; the entry guidance has no lift model, and that is stated
**Context:** "finish what is not done" left one large item: the ship stops at orbit (ADR-039). Bringing it back needs a deorbit burn, an entry on the flaps, a flip and a landing burn, and a catch by the tower.
**Decision:** (1) A new phase kind `belly` (`core/include/tfc/mission.hpp`): the body axis held `alpha` degrees from the velocity toward the vertical, the angle steered by the predicted miss along the track (the glide's and boost-back's predictor, with the phase's own ballistic coefficient) between two limits, and a bank (the whole attitude rolled about the velocity) steered by the predicted miss across it. (2) Mixers carry a `trim_deg` per surface channel: the flaps are held at 60 degrees and steer about it, so the design's travel is the smaller of the two ways from the trim. (3) A `landing.main_propellant_kg` builds the main body's catch height as a stage's is built (one helper, `detail::set_catch_height`, for both). (4) The ignition end now also requires the altitude to be under the height asked (+ the catch height): a vehicle on the far side of the planet is "below" the site along the site's up and lit its landing burn in orbit (found when the ship's belly phase ended at once at 239 km). (5) `tools/vehicle/gen_starship_return.py` writes two missions: `starship_return.json` as before (the ship stays in orbit, the booster is caught; what CI runs), and `starship_ship_return.json` with the ship's return, **marked experimental in its header**. (6) The ship's pins are 40 m from its aft end, the flaps are four channels that start stowed and are deployed by the `deploy_surfaces` event.
**Measured:** the burn of 6 s at 40 % of the three vacuum engines takes 104 m/s off 7,750 m/s; the angle of attack held at 48, 68 and 86 degrees moves the landing point by only 95 km in all (160, 232 and 254 km short of the tower at the same burn time), while one second of delay in the burn moves it by 5.5 km, so the timing aims and the angle trims; with the prediction's ballistic coefficient scanned over 100, 500, 1,000 and 2,000 kg/m^2 the miss fell 162, 70, 28 and 17 km, which says the prediction is wrong in a way that a number absorbs without being right. The committed flight comes down **14.8 km along and 7 km across the track from the tower** at 83 m/s and is a crash. The booster's catch is unchanged (seed 0: 0.5 m; the ship's flight is after it).
**Alternatives:** tune until one seed lands (a fitted number presented as a guidance: the result would not survive another seed); drop the code (the plant, the mission and the viewer already carry the flaps, and the entry is the part that works); put the ship's return in the verified mission with a lax expectation (the CI would then say the mission is fine when the ship crashes).
**Consequences:** the experimental mission takes about 80 s to design and fly on a release build (the design's probes of the longer flight); the committed mission is unchanged in time; the core gained a phase kind with its tests (810 C++ tests).
**Revisit:** a lift model in the predictor (the lift to drag ratio of the plate at its angle and the bank's cosine on the vertical), a flip phase, a landing burn whose planned deceleration is the ship's own, and the ship's catch pins on the viewer's arms; then the ship's return moves into the verified mission.

## ADR-045 (accepted 11 Oct 2026): The grid fins are drawn parallel to the ground, 1.5 m above the catch pins; the ship's flaps are flat plates in the outline of the pictures
**Context:** the owner, after ADR-043 and ADR-044: the booster's grid fins "need to be parallel to the floor so they can catch the booster, right now they are perpendicular… they open when the booster gets close to the pad", and "the Starship fins look horrible, look at pictures of the Starship", with a description (flat, thick, trapezoidal plates; the aft flaps large and swept, the forward ones smaller and sharper; low-profile hinges). What was drawn: a grid fin as a vane in the plane of the axis and the radius, edge-on to a stage falling engines first (it read as a small plate sticking out, and as shut from most views); a flap as a curved shell wrapped round the hull and stood up at 60 or 90 degrees, a scoop. The fins did deploy at the start of the boost-back (154 s, 56 km), not at the pad: what the owner saw near the pad was the camera getting close to a fin that was edge-on.
**Decision:** (1) A grid fin's face is across the axis: `chord_m` along the tangent, `span_m` along the radius, `depth` 0.7 m along the axis, a square lattice whose cells open along the axis. The plant already treated it so (its coefficients are on the frame area and the deflection swings the cells' axis toward the tangent): only the picture and the comments said otherwise; the fold is now about the tangent and lays the fin along the skin toward the tail. (2) The fins move to `x_hinge_m` 62.5 in `starship.json` and both mission files, the pins staying at `catch_pin_x_m` 61.0, and the pod carries the pin 1.5 m under the fin: the fins rest on the arms. (3) A ship's flap is a flat plate (`flatFlapGeometry`) in a trapezoidal outline with a swept leading edge and cut corners, large and swept for the aft pair, smaller and more pointed for the forward, with texture coordinates so that the tiles and the weld lines are in the plate's scale; the hinge is a barrel with two brackets and a low half-shell fairing (the actuator housing and the rods are gone).
**Measured:** the catch on seeds 0 to 99 is 98 of 100 with the fins moved (the misses are seeds 42 and 92, as before ADR-045: the 1.5 m lever on three fins did not change the booster's return).

## ADR-049 (accepted 17 Oct 2026): Wind only where there is air, and a real day's wind at the launch site
**Context:** the owner, in the viewer: "why does it say there is wind in space", and "there could be crosswind, we can use actual geographical conditions for the launches". Two causes of the first. The Aero lens drew the vehicle's velocity through the air as an arrow labelled WIND with a number (7,600 m/s in orbit), whether or not there was air; and the plant itself added the scenario's turbulence and gusts at any altitude (a test asserted that the wind at 100 km included the turbulence), held a scenario's wind profile out to the top, and gave the Moon the Earth's jet stream. The second: the wind was one direction (crossrange) at every height with a built-in speed profile, so a crosswind that turns with height, a tailwind jet or no wind at all could not be flown.
**Decision:** (1) `Vehicle6::air_presence(h)` multiplies the whole wind (mean, gusts, turbulence): 1 above 1 % of the surface density, 0 below 0.01 %, a smooth step in the logarithm between, 0 without air. (2) `scenario.wind_profile_enu` `[[altitude, east, north], ...]` is read in the site's east and north and put in the launch frame by the azimuth (Y = cos(az) N + sin(az) E, Z = sin(az) N - cos(az) E); it replaces `wind_profile` and `wind_direction`; the file reader refuses altitudes that do not increase. (3) `tools/weather/fetch_sounding.py` writes a real balloon's wind (NOAA IGRA v2, Cape Kennedy, Brownsville for Starbase, Vandenberg, Wallops) as `vehicles/weather/<site>-<time>.json`; five days are committed (the strongest winds below 20 km of 2026 at Starbase, Cape Kennedy and Vandenberg, and the newest at Starbase and Cape Kennedy). (4) The console's Vehicle tab has a *Weather* chooser (`scenario.weather`) that puts a day in the vehicle's scenario for the preview and the rig; the plant only, no flight computer is rebuilt. (5) The viewer: the arrow is AIRFLOW with a number in air and VELOCITY "(no air)" above Knudsen 1; the streamlines, shock, transonic cloud, Reynolds number, stagnation temperature and nose heating are not drawn there; the Atmosphere lens shows the tailwind and the crosswind the profile has at the vehicle's height (the spec's air table gained `wind_down_ms` and `wind_cross_ms`). (6) The demo flight is regenerated in the Starbase day of 26 January 2026 (68 m/s at 13 km, 48 m/s of it across the launch azimuth).
**Measured** (`tfc_mission`, seed 0, pad 1500; all of the wind is the only change): the built-in profile before, orbit 236.0 x 257.9 km, booster caught 1.4 m from the site with 335,811 kg left; after (the thinning from 32 km changes the built-in profile's 5 m/s at 40 km to 4.3 m/s), 234.9 x 258.5 km, caught 1.4 m, 335,874 kg. With a real day, launch azimuth 90: Starbase 26 Jan (70 m/s jet at 13 km) 229.2 x 255.9 km, **caught 1.1 m**; Starbase 16 Oct (21.8 m/s) 233.7 x 257.3 km, caught 0.3 m; Vandenberg 19 Feb (83 m/s at 10 km) 233.4 x 257.5 km, caught 0.8 m; Cape Kennedy 1 Feb (81 m/s at 9.9 km) 233.9 x 257.4 km, **crashed 4.7 m from the site** (a catch is within 1.5 m): the booster's return is not robust to the winter jet at the Cape, one day, one seed. 814 C++ tests before the change, the only failure the test that asserted the wind in space; with the new tests 816 pass.
**Alternatives:** scale the turbulence and the gusts by altitude only (a fixed 30 to 60 km: wrong on Mars and the Moon, which the density fade gets right); leave the built-in profile alone and fade only the new things (the oracle flights stay bit for bit, but the viewer's wind at 40 km would disagree with the plant's); embedded monthly climatology tables (the owner chose real soundings; they would be numbers written by me from memory); ERA5 through a web service (the archive endpoint answers pressure levels with nulls, the forecast one has only recent days; the balloon's own measurements are better evidence and keep the heights the sounding reports).
**Consequences:** the built-in profile above 32 km is thinner than before (measured above); a profile is one column of air for the whole flight (the booster's return 30 km downrange meets the Starbase wind); Starbase's balloon is at Brownsville, 35 km from the pad; above the balloon's last level (20 to 35 km) the wind is the last one held and thinned.
**Revisit:** a dispersion study of the booster catch over many real days (the Cape's jet is one failure out of four); mutation testing of the new code (not run); the wind's change with downrange distance; a day's temperature and pressure as well as wind.

## ADR-050 (accepted 17 Oct 2026): An Euler flow solver for the vehicle's body, offline and live, shown in the 3D viewer
**Context:** the owner: "start working on the full CFD" in the 3D simulation, answered (asked which) as **both, offline first**: an offline solver on the vehicle's geometry, then a live one in the viewer. Until now the air was an engineering build-up (Barrowman, modified Newtonian: `AERODYNAMICS.md`) and the viewer's flow pictures were illustrations (slender-body potential streamlines, a Taylor-Maccoll cone shock). None is a flow solution, and the build-up's normal-force slope is the same 2.0 at every Mach number.
**Decision:** (1) **`sim/cfd/`**, header only: the Euler equations of a perfect gas in 3D on a Cartesian grid (a tensor product of graded lines of faces), the body immersed in it as a signed distance with **ghost cells** that mirror the fluid at their image points (the slip wall); HLLC with HLL on a strong shock, MUSCL with the van Albada limiter, two-stage Runge-Kutta at a local time step; a persistent thread pool whose result does not depend on the number of threads (bit for bit, tested); the force from the pressure read at 0.5 and 1 cell off the surface elements and extrapolated to the surface; `gasdyn.hpp` (normal and oblique shocks, Taylor-Maccoll, Rayleigh pitot, Toro's exact Riemann solution) written apart from the solver as the thing it is checked against. (2) **`tools/sim/tfc_cfd.cpp`**: `--validate` (Sod, wedge, cones, a sphere, with the errors and a verdict), a flow on a vehicle's body (CA of the skin and of the base apart, CN, CN-alpha, the centre of pressure), `--table` (CA, CN-alpha and x_cp by Mach number in the form of `aero.table`), `--field` (the flow for the viewer); `tools/cfd/make_library.py` runs a Mach x angle-of-attack grid; `tests/test_cfd.cpp` (9 tests) and a `cfd_validate_quick` ctest (off by default: `-DTFC_CFD_VALIDATE=ON`, since the CI build's sanitizers make it too slow). (3) **The viewer**: a *CFD* view in the Aerodynamics lens: a section through the flow (Mach, pressure, or a schlieren picture) and the solver's pressure on the skin for the library case nearest the flight, with its CA, CN, CN-alpha and centre of pressure beside the build-up's; the console serves the files (`cfdlib.py`, `/api/viewer/cfd`, only names from the list); two cases are committed in `console/demo/`. (4) **A live solver on the graphics card** (`gpuflow.js`): the axisymmetric Euler equations as WebGL2 fragment shaders, the same scheme as (1) in two dimensions, following the Mach number of the flight, drawn as the same section. (5) The simulator's *flight* does not use any of it.
**Measured (17 Oct 2026):** Sod's tube: L1 density error 0.0062, 0.0033, 0.0018 at 100, 200, 400 cells; a 10-degree wedge at Mach 2: pressure behind the shock 0.00 to 0.02 % off and the shock angle 0.1 % off at 80 cells; a 15-degree cone at Mach 2.5 against Taylor-Maccoll: surface pressure 0.5 to 0.8 % off at 5 to 11 cells across its radius, shock angle 1.6 to 2.2 %; a 10-degree cone at Mach 5: 1.5 to 1.9 % and 0.5 to 0.8 %; a sphere at Mach 3: stagnation pressure -8.9 % at 10 cells and -3.1 % at 14 (the weak case of an immersed boundary). The probe at 1.5 cells that was tried first read the cone's pressure 2 to 7 % low: the pressure falls with the distance from the wall behind the shock, so it is read at 0.5 and 1 cell and extrapolated (under 1 % at every resolution). The Starship-class body at Mach 3 and 4 degrees: CN-alpha 3.05, 2.94, 2.90, 2.90 per radian and the centre of pressure 111.2, 113.8, 113.8, 116.1 m from the tail at 95,000, 177,000, 353,000 and 735,000 cells (14 to 152 s on twelve cores). The library (246,000 cells a case): CN-alpha 2.61, 2.97, 3.69, 3.74, 3.37 at Mach 2, 3, 5, 8, 12 against the build-up's 2.0, and the centre of pressure 115.7, 113.7, 97.2, 88.8, 90.1 m against its 116.9 m; the force is not linear in the angle (CN 0.555 at Mach 2 and 1.02 at Mach 3 at 10 degrees: the cross-flow Mach number passes a cylinder's critical value). The live solver on the page, a GTX 1660 Super: the same cones to 0.4 % and 1.9 % in pressure and 0.6 % in shock angle, about 11,000 iterations a second at 23,000 cells. **The robustness of the flight to it** (the plant's CN-alpha scaled, `tfc_mission`, 40 GNSS seeds): the ship's orbit is unaffected up to 2.5 x (perigee 221 to 244 km at 1.0, 1.5 and 1.9), and the booster is caught 40 of 40 at 1.0 x, **4 of 40 at 1.5 x and 9 of 40 at 1.9 x** (misses 2 to 4.7 m against a 1.5 m capture radius). 825 C++ tests pass, the viewer's selftest passes (72 of 72), the Python suite passes (596, 42 skipped), and the structural-coverage gates hold (the simulator 99.4 % of lines, 90.2 % of branches).
**Alternatives:** OpenFOAM or SU2 (neither is installed, and a body-fitted mesh of a vehicle with flaps is its own project; the Cartesian immersed boundary is the method of Cart3D, which made the Shuttle's and the SLS's aerodynamic databases, and needs no mesh); a body-fitted structured grid (better at a stagnation point, but the axis, the base and the flaps need patches); a viscous solver first (skin friction and separation are the larger physics, but a viscous boundary layer on a Cartesian grid needs a wall model or a grid ten times finer: the inviscid solver was the part that could be validated against exact solutions); a 2D live solver only (it is what the live one is, because the card is not the place for 700,000 cells at 60 frames a second); interpolating the library for a live flow in 3D (a blend of two shocks is not a shock).
**Consequences:** the repository gains about 3,300 lines (the solver, the tool, the viewer's side) and 9 C++ tests and 6 Python ones; the build has a target `tfc_cfd` (-O3 in every build type); the unit-test binary is built with the sanitisers and runs the solver 6 to 8 times slower, which is why the unit tests are on grids of a few thousand cells and the accuracy is in `--validate` and the ctest; the viewer has a new view and a new route; `console/cfd/` (the library, about 11 MB of 0.7 MB cases) is git-ignored. **The CFD and the build-up disagree** (CN-alpha 1.3 to 1.9 times, the centre of pressure 20 to 30 m forward at hypersonic speed), and nothing here says which is nearer the real vehicle, whose outline in the file is itself an estimate: this is a second answer and not a correction. The solution has no base pressure (reported apart, +0.06 to +0.09 of CA at Mach 3), no skin friction, no real gas and no flaps or fins.
**Revisit:** feed the CFD table into a flight as a measured experiment (the booster's catch over the seeds, the ship's orbit: `aero.table` is used today only by a vehicle with no sections); flaps and grid fins in the geometry (a `Box` shape from the surface table), which matter most for the ship's entry; a viscous term and a wall model; a base-pressure model; real-gas thermodynamics for the hypersonic entry; a library for the ship alone and the booster alone (the library is of the whole stack); adaptive refinement at shocks; mutation testing of `sim/cfd`; an angle of attack in the live solver (a second plane, or the 3D solver on the card at 100,000 cells).

## ADR-051 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): The flight flies on the flow solver's tables, with a real gas, a boundary layer, flaps and grid fins as geometry
**Context:** the owner, after the merge of ADR-050: "continue improving it. Add the skin friction, wake or real gas effects, and the flaps and grid fins. Also make sure the flight itself uses it. Add anything else that is missing." The Euler solver of ADR-050 was a second opinion beside the engineering build-up: it had no friction, no wake, no real gas and no flaps or fins, and nothing in the flight read it.
**Decision:** (1) **Real gas** (`sim/cfd/eos.hpp`, `Gas`): the Lighthill-Freeman ideal dissociating gas in equilibrium (vibration 3,000 K, dissociation 59,500 K), tables of 500 x 1,400 points with the exact equations outside them (2.4e-4 against them), through one abstraction that the perfect gas is also an instance of; the temperature solve is a safeguarded Newton, because the plain one failed to converge in the dissociating wake at Mach 20. (2) **The boundary layer** (`sim/cfd/viscous.hpp`): the reference-temperature method (Eckert; Meador and Smart) on flat-plate laws, Mangler's length for a body of revolution, a transition, the Reynolds analogy and a wall in radiative equilibrium, from the solution's state at the wall; **the base drag** is the build-up's law (0.25 / M, less with the engines on) and the solver's own base pressure is kept apart. (3) **Flaps** as pointed plates and **grid fins** as porous lattices (momentum sinks, `Porous`) in the geometry, force by part. (4) **`aero.cfd`**: tables of CA (pressure), CN, Cm, the Euler force on the flat ends (and on a flat forward end alone) by Mach number (2 to 20) and angle of attack (0 to 180) for each configuration of the stages (stack, ship, booster), made by `tools/cfd/make_aerodb.py` (the perfect gas to Mach 5, the real gas from Mach 8 at the altitude of a lifting entry), inlined in the vehicle files by `tools/vehicle/gen_starship_return.py` (on by default, `--no-aerodb` off), read by `Vehicle6` in place of the build-up's body force and moment at Mach 1.4 and up (a smooth fade between 1.4 and 2), with the friction and the base added by the plant, the flaps, fins and the body's turning kept from the build-up, the design tools (`divergence`, `aero_summary`) following the table, and a stage flown alone taking the table that names it alone. (5) The solver's flaps and fins are **not** used in the flight: their forces are not grid-converged.
**Measured (18 Oct 2026):** the real-gas shock behind a wall at Mach 5 and 10: pressure 0.00 %, density 1.5 and 0.7 %, temperature 1.5 and 0.3 % from the exact; the viscous layer against the exact laminar similarity solution 0.02 % (Blasius), 0.3 % (Mach 2), 1.8 % (Mach 5), against Van Driest II 1.4, 5.8 and 10.5 % (Mach 2, 5, 8). The sphere at Mach 20, real gas: stagnation pressure 8.9 % low (the perfect gas 4.0 %): the weakest check. The ship at Mach 12, 40 degrees, 52 km: friction adds CA 0.056 to 0.352, the peak heating 196 kW/m^2 off the nose. The stack's CN-alpha from the tables is 2.6 to 3.6 (the build-up's 2.0) and its centre of pressure 86 m at Mach 8 and above (the build-up's 117). The flaps: the plant's plates give an aft-flap CN of 0.005 at Mach 3 and 4 degrees; the solver 0.044 at 1 m cells and 0.092 at 0.5 m: not converged. The whole mission over 40 noise seeds: the build-up 39 of 40 caught (median miss 0.5 m) and 40 of 40 in orbit; the tables 39 of 40 caught (median 0.5 m) and 40 of 40 in orbit. **Before two fixes to the design** (below) it was 40 of 40 for the build-up and 37 of 40 for the tables, and in the experimental ship-return mission file 0 of 40 for the tables.
**Two fixes in the design, found by flying it** (they change the baseline): (a) `MissionWorld` stops probing a body that is caught, landed or crashed: the sixteen-point gain track of the booster's landing burn had been spread over the time it sat on the tower (211 s in the main mission, 3,850 s in the experimental one), and with the tables the booster there was lost 40 times in 40; (b) with that fixed the landing loop's bandwidth, which had been tuned on the accident, is scanned and set to 2.0 rad/s (it was 1.0; 1.5 to 3.0 all catch 19 of 20; at 1.0 and the fix, 38 of 40 with the build-up and 33 of 40 with the tables). The baseline changes with it: its median miss goes from 0.8 m to 0.5 m and it loses one catch in 40. (Also found by flying: a stage flown alone took the stack's table, which names it too; it now takes the table that names it alone.)
**Alternatives:** keep the flight on the build-up and use the solver only as a check (the first version; the owner asked for the flight to use it); one table for the whole vehicle (a stage that has left has its own aerodynamics, and the booster without the ship has a flat end the stack does not); the solver's flap and fin increments in the tables (their forces change by a factor of two when the cell halves, so they would put a number in the flight that the solver does not support); a viscous (RANS) solver (a research code; the friction by an integral law is right to a few per cent on a slender body); the solver run inside the flight (milliseconds against minutes: tables are what Cart3D's users fly on).
**Consequences:** the repository gains the three vehicle tables (10 KB), their inlined copies in the three generated vehicle files (each grows by 11 KB, to 28 to 36 KB), `tools/cfd/make_aerodb.py` (hours of CPU to remake the tables, cached by case), the `viscous.hpp`, `eos.hpp`, `vehicle_surfaces.hpp` and `cfd_aero.hpp` headers, 7 C++ tests in `test_cfd.cpp`, 3 in `test_aero.cpp` (and 4 more checks in the old ones), and a section 6 (the viscous layer) in `--validate`. **The flight changes**: the ship reaches orbit at 224 to 243 km by 253 to 265 km, the booster's landing burn is later and leaves it more propellant, and **the experimental ship return, which was already not a landing, comes down 100 km further off** (129 km from the site against 26): its guidance was tuned to the build-up. Anyone who changes the vehicle's outline must make the tables again (`make_aerodb.py`), or the tables fly a vehicle that is not the one in the file; the outline is not checked against them.
**Revisit:** a finer or adaptive grid and converged flap and fin increments (then in the tables); the entry guidance tuned for the solver's aerodynamics; Mach numbers 0.8 to 1.4 (a transonic correction or a different method); finite-rate chemistry; a check that the tables belong to the outline in the file (a hash); the viewer's heating map and flaps in the CFD view.

## ADR-053 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): A loads envelope in every mission report, and what it found
**Context:** the owner asked what is missing, from the point of view of the field. A trajectory is reviewed against its extremes (maximum dynamic pressure, maximum q alpha, the accelerations, the heating) before anything else, and the simulator did not report them: the Loads lens of the viewer draws them live, from a lumped-mass estimate, and nothing in the mission tool did.
**Decision:** `sim::Envelope` (`mission_world.hpp`): for each body, at every tenth frame (10 Hz), the extremes of q and the time, **q sin(alpha)** (q alpha for the small angles of an ascent; zero tail-first, where a stage coming down on its engines is not loaded across), the aerodynamic normal force, the angle of attack where q is over 1 kPa, the accelerations along and across the axis, the Mach number, the nose's stagnation flux (Sutton-Graves) and the skin's equilibrium temperature, and the aerodynamic moment about the centre of mass. `tfc_mission` prints it under each body. A test flies the rocket-recovery mission and checks the extremes against the same samples taken from outside.
**Measured (`starship_return.json`, seed 0):** the stack, then the ship: maximum q 30.6 kPa at T+65 s; maximum q sin(alpha) 4.4 kPa at T+70 s (about 250 kPa degrees, with an angle of attack of 8 degrees at that q); aerodynamic normal force up to 1.2 MN; 7.2 g along the axis at the end of the burn. **The booster on its way down: maximum q 183 kPa at T+320 s** (Mach 4.7, 600 m/s at 3.3 km), 2.8 MN across, 8.3 g. Figures of the field that I remember, and have not checked: the maximum q of a launcher on the way up is 25 to 40 kPa (this is in it), the q alpha of a launcher is held under about 3 to 5 kPa (this is at the top of it, flown on a fixed pitch program with no load relief), and a returning booster is not taken to more than a few tens of kPa. **So the booster's descent, which is flown and caught, is very likely much harder on the structure than a real one: nothing in the design looks at the loads, and the catch (39 of 40 seeds) is not evidence that the descent could be flown.** That is a finding of the instrument, not a result.
**Alternatives:** limits in the vehicle file and a failed run when they are crossed (the right next step, once there are numbers from a source to put in the file: today there are none, and a limit made up to pass is worse than none); sampling at every frame (twice the aerodynamic cost for the same extremes within a few percent).
**Consequences:** `MissionWorld::Body` gains an `Envelope`; the line cost is one aerodynamic evaluation in ten frames; nothing in a flight changes.
**Revisit:** limits from a source; load relief or wind biasing for the ascent; the descent's q (a longer burn, a lower entry speed, a different gate: it changes the catch); the same extremes in the Monte Carlo's report; a bending moment along the vehicle and not only the force and the moment about the centre of mass.

## ADR-054 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): The analyses a reviewer from the field asks for, what they found, and two changes to the flight they made
**Context:** the owner, after a review of the project against the practice of the field (`FUTURE_WORK.md` 6a and 6b): "start implementing everything". The items that need no board were built: stability margins, a Monte Carlo of the whole mission, a grid-convergence index, a response-time analysis of the bus, a bounded exhaustive check of the voter and fault manager, a requirement trace, and the limits and clearance reporting of ADR-053. Each tool is checked against a case worked by hand, and each found something.
**Decision:** (1) `sim/vehicle/margins.hpp` and `tfc_margins` (gain, phase and delay margins of every axis at every design probe, from the gains the computers carry; `CONTROL_LOOP.md` section 7). (2) `tfc_mission_mc` and `vehicles/mission_dispersions.json` (every dispersion at once over the whole mission; `gnss_sigma_m` joins the targets; `MONTE_CARLO.md` section 7). (3) `tools/cfd/gci.py` (`CFD.md` section 15). (4) `tools/bench/can_rta.py` (`PROTOCOL.md`, bus timing). (5) `tests/test_model_check.cpp` (17,576 sequences of three phases on the real manager, seven properties). (6) `tools/trace/check_trace.py`, CI, a baseline that may only shrink. (7) `mission.structure`: limits in the mission file and a check of them, the state of the pins at the arms, and the separation clearance. **Two changes to the flight:** (a) `fit_plan` leaves out the design probes whose thrust is under 5 % of the phase's largest (the first gain point of the ascent had been designed on the pad, with no thrust); (b) the powered descent's vertical channel has an integral of the speed error (`RECOVERY.md` section 9).
**Measured:** *margins* (Starship mission, 801 probes): pitch and yaw 38 to 64 degrees of phase and 7.7 to 18 dB of lower gain margin, the ship's roll 6 degrees and 2.9 dB (the roll effector's gains sit at their cap); *the probe fix*: the catch over the 40 GNSS seeds 39 to 40 of 40; *mission Monte Carlo*: with the assumed dispersions the stage is caught **0 of 100 times** (0 to 3.7 %), the ship reaches orbit 100 of 100, the booster's maximum q is 215 kPa at the median and 556 at the worst, and what fails is the glide's footprint (3 to 5 km at the landing gate); *the integral*: six of 22 single dispersions lost the catch before and one after, at the price of two catches in forty under the receiver's noise (40 to 38 of 40 on the seeds); *grid convergence*: the tables' normal force and centre of pressure are good to 1 to 2.5 % and 2 %, their moment to 6 to 10 %, **their axial force from the skin low by 7 % (Mach 3) and 15 % (Mach 8)**; *the bus*: every frame of the firmware's schedule meets its deadline in every jitter corner with a smallest slack of 340 us (the last resync frame), and **four resync frames miss by up to 475 us once the firmware sends the mission's propulsion and surface frames**; *the model check*: no violation in 256,000 frames (it first failed on a sequence of two faults, which is not the single-fault claim); *the trace*: 94 of 142 requirements marked as automatically verified had no test that named them, 19 still have none.
**Alternatives:** CBMC for the voter (not installed; the exhaustive search is the part of it that can be run here); a frequency-domain analysis in MATLAB (the toolbox is on this machine and the design probes are not: the analysis belongs next to the design); keeping the descent's proportional loop and its 40 of 40 (a nominal flight that a one-percent thrust error defeats is not a robust one); scaling the glide's ballistic coefficient by the mass the guidance tracks (tried, no better: the error is the atmosphere and the wind as much as the mass).
**Consequences:** `DesignReport` carries the probes; `MissionWorld::Body` gains an envelope, a clearance and the catch pass; `Vehicle6` records the state of the pins as they pass the arms; `descent.hpp` gains an integral (one more member, `reset()` clears it); the vehicle file gains an optional `mission.structure`; CMake gains `tfc_margins` and `tfc_mission_mc`; CI gains the trace step; 14 C++ tests and 12 Python ones.
**Revisit:** the roll loop's design (it has no margin and the cap is why); a landing that tolerates the glide's footprint (boost-back and glide predicting with a measured atmosphere); the resync burst's place before the mission's frames are built; allowables from a source; the fine-grid or extrapolated tables; the 19 requirements without a test.

## ADR-055 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): A flight log, a fault record, a stack guard and a report of the image's CRC in the firmware
**Context:** `FUTURE_WORK.md` 6b item 12: no hardware stack protection, no handler for a fatal fault (Zephyr's default halts and the watchdog resets with the cause in the reset log and nothing else), no check of the flash image, no record on the vehicle of what a run was doing when it stopped (the record of a run is the external logger).
**Decision:** `core/include/tfc/flightlog.hpp` (a ring of the last 128 frames with a CRC-8 for each record, and one fault record, in no-init RAM, as the reset log is); the board builds of the flight computer override `k_sys_fatal_error_handler` to write the fault record and halt; at boot the node prints the previous run's fault and last five frames and the CRC-32 of its flash image; `CONFIG_HW_STACK_PROTECTION=y` for `app` and `act` on the board.
**Measured:** both apps build for the board and `native_sim`; `check_elf.sh` passes; the app is 60.4 KB of 512; the core's tests (5, within the coverage gate) cover the ring, its wrap, a damaged record, a damaged header, the fault record, and the quantisation. **Not measured:** everything on the board (the MPU fault on an overflow, the handler's write surviving the reset, the CRC's time).
**Alternatives:** a CRC the image checks against a value written after linking (the right gate: it needs a post-link step and a release process, and it is the next step); a log in flash (wears, and a fault is the moment writing to it is least safe); the ACT node's log (it has the handler and the guard, not the ring: its state is one frame).
**Consequences:** `app/src/main.cpp` gains about 50 lines and 1.1 KB of RAM; `hw_common.hpp` gains `image_crc32`; the nucleo confs gain the stack guard.
**Revisit:** the expected CRC in the image and the refusal to join the vote on a mismatch; the log read out over the console on demand; the same ring in ACT; a test on the board that overflows a stack on purpose.

## ADR-056 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): The mission guidance allows for a planet that turns
**Context:** `FUTURE_WORK.md` 6a item 8: the plant has always been able to turn the planet (`planet.rotation_rate_rad_s`, the ground and the air going round the pole of the launch site), but the guidance took the landing site as a fixed point of the inertial frame. Flown on the Earth preset the booster missed by 95 to 126 km, because the pad had moved 400 m/s east for the six minutes of the return; the ship reached orbit, since PEG aims at an orbit and not at a place. Every mission file used a planet with no rotation, so the flaw was invisible, and a flight from a real site would have shown it first.
**Decision:** `Tables` gain `pole` and `planet_omega` (set from the vehicle's pole and the planet's rate by `build_mission`) and `Inputs` gains `epoch_s`, the mission clock (the flight function counts it from the stage's first flight frame plus the clock at the separation, `FlightFunction::set_epoch`; the world's ideal controllers read the body's time). `Mission::site_at(ahead)` turns the site about the pole (Rodrigues); the boost-back, the glide and the belly entry aim at `site_at(predicted time of flight)`, so the target is where the pad will be. `DragModel.omega` makes the descent predictor's drag act on the velocity relative to the air. The glide and the belly steer (angle of attack, dynamic pressure, brake) on the velocity relative to the air; the landing is flown on the velocity relative to the ground. With `planet_omega` zero all of it reduces to the old arithmetic. `starship_return_earth.json` (generated, one key different) is the mission on the Earth preset, with a CI step.
**Measured:** the first try, with the site moving and the glide still steering by the inertial velocity, brought the booster to within 119 m of the site and crashed it (97 m/s down, 109 degrees from upright: the angle of attack was taken against a velocity 400 m/s wrong); with the air-relative velocity in the glide and the belly the booster is caught 1.1 m from the site, the ship is in a 229 by 260 km orbit, and a 40-seed campaign (noise seeds, 15 s pad) gives 38 of 40 caught and 40 of 40 in orbit, the same as the planet that stands still. The report's distance from the site is now measured in the pad's own axes (`Vehicle6::pad_offset`), which a turning planet needed (the inertial subtraction said 221 km for a catch). Tests: `sequencer_a_turning_planet_carries_the_site...` (site, catch point, ground velocity, the miss moves by the pad's travel, the landing relative to the ground equals a landing on a still planet) and `descent_the_air_turns_with_the_planet...`.
**Alternatives:** flying in an Earth-fixed frame (the navigation, the gravity and the sensors would all change, and the voted core with them); correcting the site once at the separation (the pad moves during the whole return, and the miss grows with the time of flight).
**Consequences:** requirement TFC-SIM-020; the ship's return (`starship_ship_return.json`) still flies a planet that stands still and is still short of the site; the estimator and the GNSS shortcut (the receiver hands over the solution in the inertial frame) are as before.
**Revisit:** an Earth-rotating variant of the ship's return and of the 40-seed campaign in the dispersed Monte Carlo; the pad's velocity in the ascent's PEG target (the orbit is inertial, so it is not needed, but a launch azimuth is).

## ADR-057 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): The engines of a cluster one by one, and an analysis of the propulsion
**Context:** the owner asked for a detailed propulsion analysis of the booster and the ship, for the gimbal to work and make a difference in the simulator, and for more detailed physics. Until now a stage's engines that gimbal shared one angle (one servo), roll was done by cold-gas thrusters, an engine could not be weak or have a gimbal that fails, the specific impulse was constant, and nothing computed what the engines could do (the authority of the gimbal, the nozzle against theory, what an engine out leaves).
**Decision:** (1) A vehicle file may say `per_engine_gimbal`: every engine that gimbals has its own angles and servo (`eg_p`, `eg_y`; travel, rate and lag per engine, the second-order servo and play of the `actuator` block as before), the pitch and yaw command goes to each, and the roll command is added along each engine's tangent with `roll_gain` (differential gimbal); the force and moment are summed engine by engine as they were, so the cosine loss and the torque of a deflection are not separate formulas. (2) Per engine also: `thrust_factor`, `gimbal_fault` (`stuck` or `hardover` from a time) and `isp_throttle_loss`. (3) `sim/vehicle/propulsion.hpp` and `tfc_propulsion`: the cluster, the thrust and specific impulse against altitude, the isentropic nozzle with the chamber pressure, area ratio and gamma the file names (the plant does not read them: the analysis checks the file's thrust and exit area against an ideal nozzle), the sea-level separation throttle, the gimbal's authority at points of the burn, an engine out and the worst pair with the gimbal that trims them, and the servo's bandwidth. (4) The Starship-class files are generated with all of it (`--shared-gimbal` writes the old stage-wide gimbal); the vacuum engine's exit area is now the ideal one for its area ratio; the viewer draws each engine's own lean (`egim` in the pose).
**Measured:** 13 new tests, each a hand calculation of the geometry or the textbook, and the analysis compared with the plant (the torque per degree, the roll, the trim that cancels an engine out run in the plant). With every setting at its default the plant is the old one to rounding. The mission tools: the baseline still reaches orbit and catches the booster (0.9 m from the site); the 40-seed campaign gives 37 caught (38 without the loss of specific impulse with throttle; 37 with the stage-wide gimbal and the loss: the per-engine gimbal costs no catch). Flown: the booster's gimbal locked loses both stages; one inner engine's gimbal hard over to 3° at T+30 s is trimmed (caught); one engine out at T+50 s leaves the ship in orbit and **loses the booster's return** (it arrives inverted 0.3 to 0.5 km from the tower). The tool found that the first files' vacuum-engine exit area (4.5 m²) was 26 % off an ideal nozzle of the file's area ratio and thrust; it is 3.57 m² now. The run time rises 2 %.
**Alternatives:** keeping one angle per stage and adding roll to it (no engine could differ from its neighbour: no failure, no limit by ring); a gimbal model with the actuator's load, power and the moving engine mass (no data to set it with, and it would not change what the flight computers see); making the plant read the nozzle (the thrust formula is exact for a nozzle that does not separate, and a separated one needs data that does not exist here); bounding the landing burn's planned deceleration by the engines' thrust at the vehicle's mass to rescue the engine-out return (tried: it did not rescue it and moved the baseline, so it was removed).
**Consequences:** requirement TFC-SIM-021; `PROPULSION.md`; the roll loop now has the differential gimbal as well as the cold-gas thrusters (the design probes include it); a vehicle file that does not say `per_engine_gimbal` flies as before. The flight computers still have no engine health: a dead engine is counted.
**Revisit:** the engine-out return (an engine-health input to the landing, and the choice of which engines to light); the side load of a separated nozzle at low throttle on the way down (the margin is 1 % of throttle, `PROPULSION.md` section 3); per-engine dispersions in the Monte Carlo; the nozzle and gimbal numbers when a better source than memory is found.

## ADR-058 (accepted 18 Oct 2026): The flight computers are told which engines are out, the landing counts its engines, and an engine out no longer loses the booster
**Context:** ADR-057 found that an engine out on the way up left the ship in orbit and lost the booster's return, and that the flight computers had no engine health. The owner asked for the health input, a landing that works with a few engines out, and an ascent that reaches orbit when one of the big engines goes out, the rest gimbaling to adjust.
**Decision:** (1) **A health word** from the engine controllers (`0x509`, `0x50A`: 64 bits, bit e = engine e of the vehicle file, only growing, ORed in by each receiver), made in the simulator from the plant's truth after a delay (`engine_detect_s`, 0.15 s) and delivered to each of the three computers; the ideal pilot hears at once. (2) The sequencer takes the share of the rated thrust that is left in every burning phase (`Tables::group_engines`, `engine_thrust`) for the thrust and the flow, so that the mass it keeps, the boost-back's cut-off, the PEG's burn model and the gimbal's thrust scaling follow the truth. (3) A landing counts the engines left in its pool, caps its planned deceleration by the thrust they give (`planned_decel`), and, when an engine of the chosen option is out, asks for a *number* of engines from the whole pool (`PropCommand::engines`, byte 5 of `0x210`); the plant's engine controller runs that many of the engines that can run, those nearest the axis. (4) **Three bugs fixed**: a stage let go kept neither its engine failures nor its per-engine gimbal and its engines were not numbered as the stage alone has them; a glide that began in the engines' tail was designed on three of its 124 probes (the thrust filter of ADR-054 kept the one probe with thrust and dropped the rest), with the fins' roll authority of a thrusting flight, and the booster spun up in roll; the guidance's mass ran on the nominal flow.
**Measured** (`PROPULSION.md` section 9): any one of the booster's 33 engines out at any time from 0 to 132 s, 100 of 100 flights in orbit and 100 of 100 caught (0.6 m median miss); any one of the ship's six, 60 of 60 both; two to five out on the way up, 48 of 48 in orbit, 46 caught; one to six of the booster's engines out before the landing, 48 of 48 caught; k of the 13 landing engines out, 8 random sets each: caught 8 of 8 for k up to 6, none from 7; `starship_return_engines_out.json` (five out) caught 0.5 m from the site. The baseline is unchanged: 0.9 m, 37 of 40 seeds (the three misses arrive at the arms over the sink limit). Tests: 12 new, in the frames, the sequencer, the plant (selection, carry-over, tables), the design and the world; requirement TFC-SIM-022.
**Alternatives:** an analytic detection of an engine out from the specific force against the commanded thrust (no engine controllers needed; not built: a later cross-check); the flight computers choosing the engines (they would need the engines' geometry; they ask for a number); a fixed priority list of engines in the engine controller (the best subset by balance is a handful of lines and landed two more of ten pool cases); **scheduling the fins by dynamic pressure, giving the roll priority at a fin's stop, keying the glide's gains by altitude, and lighting the landing from higher when the engines left decelerate less were each built and measured and taken out**: the first three changed no result (the real cause was the filter) and the last made five and six engines out worse.
**Consequences:** a vehicle file with `scenario.engine_failures` now flies the failure through the whole mission, the stage included; the tables of a mission carry the engines of each group; CI flies the engines-out mission. The firmware does not send or receive the frames yet (host only, like the GNSS and propulsion frames).
**Revisit:** detection from the thrust; a partial loss of thrust as an event; a landing that survives seven or more of its thirteen engines out (a burn that can start higher, a harder deceleration); the three seeds that arrive at the arms too fast.
